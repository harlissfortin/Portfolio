# White Belt — Module Lesson Content

Source-of-truth scripts for the 12 self-paced micro-modules. Each module follows the same
pattern: **Hook → Teach → Show (vertical example) → Try (interaction) → Takeaways → Go look.**
Target narration length ≈ 6–8 minutes per module plus interaction time. The closing *Go look* is
a five-minute task done in the learner's own workplace, not at the screen; its result is an
entry in the learner's [process dossier](templates/process-dossier.md). Twelve entries make the
dossier; one of them becomes the Module 12 submission. Show examples and reflection prompts
also vary by the learner's **role** (frontline / supervisor / leader) where
[`role-variants.md`](role-variants.md) says so.

---

## Module 1 — Why Processes Fail People (15 min)

**Learning goal:** Learner can explain why most workplace frustration is caused by process
design, not by people, and can name one recurring frustration in their own work.

### Hook
Think of the last time your workday went sideways. The missing information. The system that
made you enter the same thing twice. The approval that sat in someone's inbox for three days.
Now ask: was that a *lazy person* problem — or a *badly designed process* problem?

### Teach
W. Edwards Deming, one of the founders of modern quality thinking, offered a rule of thumb
that has become famous: **about 94% of performance problems belong to the system — the way
work is designed and managed — and only about 6% to special causes, such as one person's
actions on one day.** (Deming was speaking about common-cause versus special-cause
variation; Joseph Juran's earlier estimate was 85/15. The exact number matters less than
the direction: the system dominates.) People generally come to work wanting to do a good
job. When they can't,
it's usually because the process makes the wrong thing easy and the right thing hard.

Continuous improvement (CI) starts with this mindset shift:

> **Blame the process, not the person. Then fix the process.**

This matters for two reasons:
1. **It's more accurate.** Swapping the person rarely fixes a recurring problem — the next
   person inherits the same broken process and produces the same result.
2. **It's safer to talk about.** When problems are framed as process flaws, people stop hiding
   them. A workplace where problems are hidden cannot improve.

### Show (vertical example)
- *Manufacturing:* A packer keeps shipping the wrong variant. Root issue: two near-identical
  part bins sit side by side with tiny labels. New packer, same errors.
- *Healthcare:* A nurse "forgets" a discharge step. Root issue: the discharge checklist lives
  in a different system than the one nurses chart in.
- *Office:* An analyst sends stale figures. Root issue: three "final" versions of the
  spreadsheet exist and nothing marks which is current.

### Try
Reflection prompt (typed, saved to the learner's workbook): *"Describe one recurring
frustration in your work. Rewrite it once as a person problem, and once as a process problem.
Which framing gives you something you could actually fix?"*

### Takeaways
- Most recurring problems are built into the process; changing people rarely fixes them.
- Framing problems as process flaws makes them safe to surface — the raw material of improvement.
- Your frustrations are data. This course teaches you to read them.

### Go look — dossier entry 1 (5 min, in the workplace)
Take the process version from your Module 1 reflection to the place where it happens. Stand there for five minutes and write down one thing you can see that makes the problem likely.
Saved to the learner's [process dossier](templates/process-dossier.md); never scored, never transmitted.

## Module 2 — What Is a Process? (15 min)

**Learning goal:** Learner can identify the Supplier, Input, Process, Output, and Customer
(SIPOC) of a simple process they perform.

### Teach
A **process** is any repeatable sequence of steps that turns inputs into outputs for a
customer. Making coffee is a process. Admitting a patient is a process. Closing the monthly
books is a process. If it happens more than once and has steps, it's a process — and anything
that's a process can be studied and improved.

The five-part lens we use is **SIPOC**:

| Letter | Means | Ask yourself |
|---|---|---|
| **S** — Suppliers | Who provides what the process needs? | Where do my inputs come from? |
| **I** — Inputs | Materials, information, requests | What do I need to start? |
| **P** — Process | The 4–7 major steps | What happens, at a high level? |
| **O** — Outputs | What the process produces | What leaves this process? |
| **C** — Customers | Who receives the output | Who uses what I produce — and what do they need it to be? |

Two ideas hiding inside SIPOC do a lot of work:
- **Customers are often internal.** The next department, the next shift, the next system —
  whoever receives your output is your customer, and their requirements define your quality.
- **You are someone's supplier.** Your sloppy output is someone else's defective input.

### Show
SIPOC for "process an expense report": Suppliers = employees, card provider; Inputs =
receipts, expense form; Process = submit → manager approves → finance reviews → payment
issued; Outputs = reimbursement, accounting entry; Customers = employee, finance, auditors.

### Try
Click-to-assign (select item, then column — never drag-and-drop, per the accessibility spec): learner sorts ten items (e.g., "pharmacy", "prescription", "verify insurance",
"filled prescription", "patient") into S-I-P-O-C columns for a pharmacy pickup process.

### Takeaways
- Everything repeatable is a process, and every process has suppliers, inputs, steps, outputs,
  and customers.
- Quality is defined by the customer of the output — often the next person in the chain.

### Go look — dossier entry 2 (5 min, in the workplace)
For the process you touch most: name its customer (the next person who receives your output) and one thing they need from it to be "good".
Saved to the learner's [process dossier](templates/process-dossier.md); never scored, never transmitted.

## Module 3 — Where This Came From: TPS, Deming, and Variation (15 min)

**Learning goal:** Learner can state in one sentence each what Lean and Six Sigma contribute,
and explain why variation is "the enemy."

### Teach
Two improvement traditions merged to form Lean Six Sigma.

**Lean** grew from the **Toyota Production System (TPS)**, built in post-war Japan when Toyota
could not afford warehouses of inventory or armies of inspectors. Its core question:
*"Of everything we do, what does the customer actually value — and how do we eliminate the
rest?"* Lean is the discipline of **removing waste and making work flow.**

**Six Sigma** was born at Motorola in the 1980s and scaled at GE. Its core question:
*"Why doesn't the process give the same good result every time?"* Six Sigma is the discipline
of **reducing variation using data.** The name comes from a statistical goal: a process so
consistent that defects are vanishingly rare (about 3.4 per million opportunities).

Why is **variation** the enemy? Because customers experience the *spread*, not the average.
A café whose espresso takes "3 minutes on average" but ranges from 1 to 9 minutes feels
unreliable. A lab that returns results in 2–14 days can't be planned around. Averages hide
the pain; variation *is* the pain.

**Deming's contribution** ties them together: improve the system using data and the
**Plan-Do-Check-Act** learning cycle, and treat quality as management's responsibility, not
the inspector's. (The cycle originated with Walter Shewhart; Deming himself insisted on
calling it Plan-Do-**Study**-Act — "study," because the point of the third step is to
learn what happened, not merely to check a box. This program uses the more common PDCA
label and means it Deming's way.)

Together: **Lean makes work flow; Six Sigma makes results consistent.** Speed without
consistency is chaos delivered faster; consistency without speed is reliable disappointment.
You need both.

### Try
3-question check, including: *"A hospital lab averages 24 hours for results, but individual
results range from 4 to 96 hours. Which discipline most directly targets the 4-to-96 problem?"*

### Takeaways
- Lean = remove waste, create flow (from Toyota).
- Six Sigma = reduce variation with data (from Motorola/GE).
- Customers feel variation, not averages.

### Go look — dossier entry 3 (5 min, in the workplace)
Find one thing in your work that varies from day to day — arrival time, order size, how long a step takes. Write down the range you have seen.
Saved to the learner's [process dossier](templates/process-dossier.md); never scored, never transmitted.

## Module 4 — Value-Added vs. Non-Value-Added Work (15 min)

**Learning goal:** Learner can apply the three-part value test to classify activities as
value-added, non-value-added, or necessary non-value-added.

### Teach
An activity is **value-added (VA)** only if it passes **all three** of these tests:

1. **The customer would pay for it** (or cares that it happened).
2. **It physically or informationally changes the thing** being worked on.
3. **It's done right the first time** (rework never counts, even rework of a value step).

Everything else is **non-value-added (NVA)** — waste. One important subcategory:
**necessary non-value-added (NNVA)**: things the customer doesn't value but that regulation,
safety, or current business reality requires (compliance documentation, some inspections,
payroll processing). You minimize NNVA; you eliminate NVA; you protect and improve VA.

The startling finding, consistent across industries: in a typical unimproved process, **less
than 10% of elapsed time is value-added.** A mortgage that takes 30 days usually contains a
few hours of actual work; the rest is waiting, handoffs, checking, and rework. This is great
news — it means enormous improvement is available without asking anyone to work faster.

### Show
Classify steps in "get a blood test": drawing the sample (VA), labeling per protocol (NNVA),
patient waiting 40 minutes (NVA), re-drawing because the first tube was mislabeled (NVA —
rework), analyzing the sample (VA).

### Try
Click-to-assign sort: 12 activities from the learner's chosen vertical sorted into
VA / NVA / NNVA, with instant feedback explaining each.

### Takeaways
- Value is defined by the customer, changes the work item, and is done right the first time.
- Most process time — often 90%+ — is non-value-added. That's the opportunity, not the shame.
- Necessary NVA is minimized; pure NVA is eliminated.

### Go look — dossier entry 4 (5 min, in the workplace)
Watch one work item for five minutes. Estimate how much of that time something was actually *done* to it.
Saved to the learner's [process dossier](templates/process-dossier.md); never scored, never transmitted.

### Cumulative check (spaced retrieval)

Two items drawn from the PRAC pool re-testing **Modules 1–2**: whose requirements define the
quality of your output, and process-versus-person attribution. Feedback gives the rationale.
Not scored toward certification — this is retrieval practice, and getting one wrong here is
the most useful thing that can happen to a learner at this point.

## Module 5 — The 8 Wastes, Part 1: D-O-W-N (20 min)

**Learning goal:** Learner can define Defects, Overproduction, Waiting, and Non-utilized
talent, and recognize each in a workplace scene.

### Teach
The 8 wastes spell **DOWNTIME**. This module covers the first four.

**D — Defects.** Work that is wrong, incomplete, or unusable — and everything that follows
from it: rework, scrap, corrections, apologies, credit notes. Defects are the most expensive
waste because they multiply: one wrong entry can trigger hours of downstream cleanup.
*Examples:* a machined part out of spec; a medication order with the wrong dose; an invoice
with the wrong purchase-order number.

**O — Overproduction.** Making more, sooner, or faster than the next step needs. Toyota calls
it the worst waste because it *manufactures the other wastes*: overproduced items must be
stored (inventory), moved (transportation), and often fixed later (defects).
*Examples:* running a big batch "while the machine is set up"; printing 50 report copies for
12 attendees; generating weekly reports nobody reads.

**W — Waiting.** People or work items idle — waiting for approvals, information, machines,
meetings, or the previous step. Waiting is usually the single largest component of lead time.
*Examples:* patients in a waiting room; a job stalled in an approval queue; a crew waiting on
materials.

**N — Non-utilized talent.** The waste added when Lean moved from 7 wastes to 8: failing to
use people's skills, knowledge, and ideas. The operator who knows why the line jams but is
never asked. The bilingual clerk doing data entry while the team pays interpreters.
*This is the waste that CI itself fixes* — a belt program is, at its core, an attack on
non-utilized talent.

### Try
Spot-the-waste video 1: a 3-minute walkthrough scene (vertical-specific); learner clicks
moments where D, O, W, or N wastes appear; each click reveals the classification and why.

### Takeaways
- DOWN: Defects, Overproduction, Waiting, Non-utilized talent.
- Overproduction breeds other wastes; defects multiply cost downstream; waiting dominates
  lead time; unused ideas are the most human waste of all.

### Go look — dossier entry 5 (5 min, in the workplace)
Find one example of **Waiting** you can see from where you stand. Who or what waits, and for what?
Saved to the learner's [process dossier](templates/process-dossier.md); never scored, never transmitted.

## Module 6 — The 8 Wastes, Part 2: T-I-M-E (20 min)

**Learning goal:** Learner can define Transportation, Inventory, Motion, and
Extra-processing, and distinguish transportation from motion.

### Teach
**T — Transportation.** Moving the *work item* (product, patient, document, file) between
locations or systems. Every move adds delay and damage/loss risk, and none adds value.
*Examples:* parts trucked between buildings; a patient transported to a scanner across the
campus; a request re-entered from one system into another.

**I — Inventory.** Work piling up between steps: raw materials, work-in-progress, finished
goods — and their office equivalents: full inboxes, ticket backlogs, unread reports. Inventory
ties up cash and space, hides problems (a big pile means nobody notices one bad item), and
goes stale.
*Examples:* a month of raw material "just in case"; 400 unresolved tickets; charts awaiting
coding.

**M — Motion.** Movement of the *person* doing the work: reaching, walking, searching,
clicking through six screens, alt-tabbing between systems. Motion wears people out without
advancing the work.
*Rule of thumb:* **Transportation moves the work; Motion moves the worker.**
*Examples:* a nurse walking 4 miles per shift hunting supplies; an operator reaching across
the line for tools; a rep navigating 5 systems per call.

**E — Extra-processing.** Doing more than the customer requires: polishing beyond the spec,
double- and triple-checking, gathering signatures that add no scrutiny, filling fields nobody
reads, gold-plating reports.
*Examples:* deburring surfaces the customer never sees; three approvals where one decides;
a beautiful 30-slide deck when the decision needed one number.

### Try
Spot-the-waste video 2 (same scene, continued), plus a paired-scenario item bank:
"Transportation or Motion?" ×4.

### Takeaways
- TIME: Transportation, Inventory, Motion, Extra-processing.
- Transportation moves the work; motion moves the worker.
- Inventory hides problems; extra-processing is effort the customer never asked for.

### Go look — dossier entry 6 (5 min, in the workplace)
Find one example of **Motion** (a person moving) and one of **Transportation** (a work item moving).
Saved to the learner's [process dossier](templates/process-dossier.md); never scored, never transmitted.

### Cumulative check (spaced retrieval)

Two items re-testing **Modules 3–4**: which discipline targets a wide spread of outcomes, and
the three value-added tests.

## Module 7 — Waste in Your World (15 min)

**Learning goal:** Learner can identify at least 5 of the 8 wastes in their own industry
context and articulate why each matters to the customer.

### Teach (vertical-specific — three variants share this skeleton)
This module replays all 8 wastes in the learner's own environment with a "day in the life"
narrative. Each waste appears twice: once obviously, once subtly.

*Manufacturing variant anchor story:* one order for a machined bracket follows the full path
from order entry to shipment; learners track wastes accumulating against a running clock —
final reveal: 11 days elapsed, 47 minutes value-added.

*Healthcare variant anchor story:* one patient's outpatient visit from booking to results
call-back — final reveal: 31 days elapsed, 34 minutes of clinical value time.

*Transactional variant anchor story:* one customer's invoice dispute from email to resolution
— final reveal: 19 days elapsed, 22 minutes of actual investigation.

The closing teach: the running-clock reveal *is* the business case for improvement, and every
learner is standing next to a story like this in their own workplace.

### Try
Vertical scenario quiz: 8 scenarios, learner names the dominant waste; distractors are
designed around the classic confusions (transportation/motion, inventory/overproduction).

### Takeaways
- All 8 wastes exist in every industry; only the costumes change.
- Elapsed time vs. value time is the most persuasive improvement story you can tell.

### Go look — dossier entry 7 (5 min, in the workplace)
Take the anchor story's elapsed-vs-value question to one of your own items: how long does it take start to finish, and how much of that is work?
Saved to the learner's [process dossier](templates/process-dossier.md); never scored, never transmitted.

## Module 8 — 5S: A Place for Everything (15 min)

**Learning goal:** Learner can name the five S's in order and explain what each contributes,
including the digital-workspace application.

### Teach
**5S** is the entry-level Lean method for organizing a workspace so that problems become
visible and normal work becomes effortless.

1. **Sort** (*Seiri*) — Remove what isn't needed. Red-tag doubtful items; when in doubt,
   move it out.
2. **Set in Order** (*Seiton*) — A place for everything, everything in its place. Arrange by
   frequency of use; label; outline; make the *absence* of something instantly visible.
3. **Shine** (*Seiso*) — Clean and inspect. Cleaning is inspection in disguise: wiping down
   the machine is how you notice the oil leak early.
4. **Standardize** (*Seiketsu*) — Make the first three S's the documented norm: photo
   standards, checklists, labeled layouts, so any workspace deviation is obvious.
5. **Sustain** (*Shitsuke*) — Build the habit: audits, leader walk-throughs, team ownership.
   This is where most 5S efforts die; sustainment is a leadership behavior, not a poster.

**Digital 5S** applies the same logic to shared drives, inboxes, dashboards, and code repos:
Sort (delete/archive), Set in Order (naming conventions, folder standards), Shine (fix broken
links and stale data), Standardize (templates), Sustain (periodic cleanup cadence).

5S is not housekeeping for its own sake. Its purpose is **making abnormality visible**:
in a 5S'd workspace, the missing tool, the extra part, and the growing backlog announce
themselves.

### Try
Before/after photo pairs (physical bench, supply room, shared drive): learner identifies
which S produced each visible change.

### Takeaways
- Sort, Set in Order, Shine, Standardize, Sustain.
- The point is visible abnormality, not tidiness.
- Sustain is the hard one — and it belongs to leaders and teams, not posters.

### Go look — dossier entry 8 (5 min, in the workplace)
Find one thing someone searched for today. Where was it? Where should it live so nobody searches again?
Saved to the learner's [process dossier](templates/process-dossier.md); never scored, never transmitted.

### Cumulative check (spaced retrieval)

Two items re-testing **Modules 5–6**: waste identification in a fresh scene, and the
transportation-versus-motion discriminator.

## Module 9 — DMAIC as a Storyline (20 min)

**Learning goal:** Learner can name the five DMAIC phases in order and state the one question
each phase answers.

### Teach
When a problem is too big for "just fix it," Lean Six Sigma uses a five-phase roadmap:
**DMAIC** (duh-MAY-ick). Don't memorize tools yet — memorize the *questions*:

| Phase | The question it answers | In plain terms |
|---|---|---|
| **Define** | *What problem are we solving, and why does it matter?* | Agree on the problem, the goal, the scope, and who cares. |
| **Measure** | *How bad is it, really — in numbers?* | Get trustworthy data on current performance. No baseline, no proof of improvement later. |
| **Analyze** | *What is actually causing it?* | Find and **verify** root causes with evidence — not the first plausible story. |
| **Improve** | *What change fixes the cause — and does it work?* | Generate, select, pilot, and implement solutions aimed at verified causes. |
| **Control** | *How do we make sure it stays fixed?* | Lock in the gain: standards, monitoring, response plans, handover to the owner. |

Two disciplines make DMAIC different from ordinary problem-solving:
- **No jumping to solutions.** The most common failure in workplace problem-solving is landing
  in Improve on day one. DMAIC forces the boring, decisive middle: measure and analyze first.
- **No walking away after the fix.** The second most common failure is improvement that
  evaporates in 90 days. Control is a full phase for a reason.

White Belts aren't expected to *lead* DMAIC — but you will be invited into these projects as
the people who know the real work, and knowing the storyline lets you contribute from day one.

### Try
Phase-matching exercise: 15 activities ("interview customers about requirements," "run a
pilot on one line," "set up a weekly metric review") assigned to the right phase by click-to-assign.

### Takeaways
- Define → Measure → Analyze → Improve → Control; each phase answers one question.
- The discipline is in resisting the leap to solutions and in not abandoning the fix.

### Go look — dossier entry 9 (5 min, in the workplace)
Of everything in your dossier so far, which one would need real data to understand — not a quick fix? Mark it ★.
Saved to the learner's [process dossier](templates/process-dossier.md); never scored, never transmitted.

## Module 10 — Process Problems vs. People Problems (15 min)

**Learning goal:** Given a scenario, learner can distinguish a process problem from a genuine
people problem and choose the constructive first response.

### Teach
Module 1 said "blame the process." This module makes that skill practical, because saying
"it's the process" isn't the same as knowing *how to tell*.

Ask three diagnostic questions about any recurring problem:
1. **Does it happen across different people?** If three different techs make the same error,
   the error is designed into the work.
2. **Would a conscientious new hire eventually make the same mistake?** If yes, it's the
   process. ("Set up to fail" test.)
3. **Does the process make the right way the easy way?** If the correct method requires
   heroics — extra clicks, memory, workarounds — expect failure at scale.

Genuine people problems exist — skill gaps, conduct issues — and they're handled by
management and HR, not by process tools. The tell: a genuine people problem is *specific to
the person and persists when the process is sound.* But the honest sequence is:
**rule out the process first.** Most "people problems" dissolve under that examination.

The practical payoff for a White Belt: when something goes wrong near you, your first
question changes from *"who?"* to *"what about how this is set up made that likely?"* —
asked out loud, that question changes the room.

### Try
Scenario judgment items: 6 scenarios; learner picks process problem / people problem /
can't tell yet — with "can't tell yet — go look at the process" as the correct answer for two
of them, teaching intellectual honesty.

### Takeaways
- Test: multiple people? new hire would too? is the right way the easy way?
- Rule out the process before concluding "people problem."
- "What made that likely?" beats "who did that?"

### Go look — dossier entry 10 (5 min, in the workplace)
Take the ★ item. Run the test: multiple people? would a new hire do it too? is the right way the easy way? Record the answers.
Saved to the learner's [process dossier](templates/process-dossier.md); never scored, never transmitted.

### Cumulative check (spaced retrieval)

Two items re-testing **Modules 7–8**: the elapsed-versus-value story, and the purpose of 5S.

## Module 11 — The Belt System and Your Role in CI Culture (10 min)

**Learning goal:** Learner can describe what each belt level does and identify the
contributions available to them immediately.

### Teach
The belt ladder, in one line each:
- **White Belt (you):** speaks the language; spots and reports waste; contributes ideas.
- **Yellow Belt:** serves on project teams; maps processes; runs small local improvements.
- **Green Belt:** leads a DMAIC project on a real process, part-time.
- **Black Belt:** leads major cross-functional projects; advanced statistics; coaches Green Belts.
- **Master Black Belt:** designs the whole improvement program; coaches everyone; ties CI to strategy.

The system only works because of its base. Green and Black Belts do not know where the
problems are — **you do.** Every strong CI culture runs on frontline eyes:
- **Report problems without fear** — a surfaced problem is a gift, and the culture must treat
  it that way.
- **Contribute ideas** — nobody knows the job like the person doing it.
- **Join projects when invited** — as the expert witness on how work really happens (not the
  org-chart version).
- **Protect improvements** — follow the new standard, and speak up when it isn't working
  rather than quietly reverting.

### Takeaways
- Each belt has a distinct job; the ladder runs on frontline participation.
- Your immediate contributions: surface problems, offer ideas, join projects, hold standards.

### Go look — dossier entry 11 (5 min, in the workplace)
Who in your organization could act on the ★ item? Name the role, not a person.
Saved to the learner's [process dossier](templates/process-dossier.md); never scored, never transmitted.

## Module 12 — Your First Contribution: the "See a Waste" Exercise (20 min)

**Learning goal:** Learner submits one real, well-formed waste observation from their own work.

### Teach
Certification requires one real observation — because a White Belt who has *done it once* is
different from one who watched videos. The form (see
[`templates/see-a-waste.md`](templates/see-a-waste.md)) asks for five things:

1. **Where:** the process and place.
2. **What I observed:** facts only — what you'd see on video. No blame, no causes, no
   solutions yet. ("The team re-enters order data from System A into System B" — not "IT
   never fixed the integration because they don't care.")
3. **Which waste(s):** name it from DOWNTIME; many observations contain two or three.
4. **Who it affects:** the customer of that process step and how they feel it.
5. **An idea (optional but encouraged):** one sentence, offered humbly — ideas here are
   hypotheses, not demands.

A worked example is shown in each vertical, including a *bad* example (blamey, vague,
solution-first) rewritten live into a good one — the contrast is the actual lesson.

In corporate deployments, submissions flow to a shared gallery visible to the learner's CI
team — the program's rollout dashboard counts and themes them, and this becomes the
organization's first crowd-sourced problem inventory.

### Try
Submit the exercise. Auto-checks: all required fields, observation ≥ 2 sentences,
waste category selected. (Corporate option: internal CI lead review layer.)

### Takeaways
- An observation is facts + waste name + who it hurts. Blame kills it; vagueness starves it.
- Your submission is a real contribution to a real problem inventory — the first of many.

### Go look — dossier entry 12 (5 min, in the workplace)
Choose the dossier entry you will submit. Rewrite it into the four parts: where, what I observed, which waste, who it affects.
Saved to the learner's [process dossier](templates/process-dossier.md); never scored, never transmitted. This is the entry that becomes the field exercise submission.

### Cumulative check (spaced retrieval)

Two items re-testing **Modules 9–10**: the question each DMAIC phase answers, and the
"would a new hire make the same mistake?" diagnostic. These close the course before the
knowledge check, so the learner enters the assessment having just retrieved the two
frameworks it weights most heavily.
