## Screen 1 of 8 — Was that a lazy person problem?

*On screen: the question "Was that a lazy person problem — or a badly designed process
problem?" set in large type, above the line "Think about the last time your workday went
sideways." No image.*

Think about the last time your workday went sideways. Maybe it was information you needed and
couldn't find. A system that made you type the same thing twice. An approval that sat in
somebody's inbox for three days while a customer waited.

Now hold that moment in your mind, because we're going to ask an uncomfortable question about
it. Was that a lazy person problem — or a badly designed process problem?

Most of us are trained to answer that question with a name. Someone dropped the ball. Someone
didn't check. Over the next few minutes I want to show you why that answer is usually wrong,
and why the alternative is far more useful to you.

## Screen 2 of 8 — The 94/6 rule of thumb

*On screen: the number 94% shown large, beside a horizontal proportion bar. The bar is divided
into two parts with a small gap between them: a long green part labelled "the system — how work
is designed and managed, about 94%" and a short grey part labelled "special causes — one person,
one machine, one day, about 6%".*

This is the finding that started modern quality thinking. W. Edwards Deming, working with
Japanese manufacturers after the second world war, put a number on it: about ninety-four
percent of performance problems come from the system — the way the work is designed and
managed. Only about six percent come from what he called special causes: one person, one
machine, one unusual day.

Two honesty notes before we go on. The exact numbers are a rule of thumb, not a measurement;
Joseph Juran had earlier put it at eighty-five and fifteen. And Deming was making a technical
point about two kinds of variation, which you'll meet properly at Yellow Belt.

But the direction is what matters, and on the direction the evidence has been remarkably
consistent for seventy years. When something goes wrong at work, the way the work is set up is
the overwhelming favourite. The person is the long shot.

## Screen 3 of 8 — Blame the process, not the person

*On screen: "Blame the process, not the person." in large type, with "Then fix the process."
beneath it in lighter weight. No image.*

So here is the shift that every improvement method is built on, and it fits on one line: blame
the process, not the person. Then fix the process.

I want to be careful here, because this is often misheard in two directions. It does not mean
nobody is ever accountable for anything. And it does not mean people are helpless.

What it means is narrower and more practical. When you are staring at a problem that keeps
happening, the most productive first question is not *who did this*. It is *what about the way
this work is set up made that likely?*

That question is worth learning to ask out loud, because it changes what a room does next.

## Screen 4 of 8 — Reason one: it's more accurate

*On screen: the heading "1 · It's more accurate" and the line "Swap the person, keep the process
— and the next person inherits the same result." Beside it, a diagram showing a three-step loop:
"A problem happens" leads to "The person is replaced", which leads to "The same problem
happens", and an arrow returns from there to the beginning of the loop.*

There are two reasons to work this way, and the first one is simply that it is more accurate.

Think about what happens when a recurring problem gets treated as a people problem. The person
is coached, or moved, or replaced. And then the next person arrives — conscientious,
well-intentioned, new — and inherits the same layout, the same forms, the same two systems that
don't talk to each other. Within a few weeks, the same thing goes wrong again.

That pattern is the single most reliable signal you will learn to spot. If a problem survives a
change of personnel, the problem was never in the personnel. It is built into the work, waiting
for whoever shows up next.

## Screen 5 of 8 — Reason two: it's safer to talk about

*On screen: the heading "2 · It's safer to talk about" above three lines — "Problems framed as
process flaws get reported.", "Problems framed as personal failures get hidden.", and, set
apart in emphasis, "A workplace that hides problems cannot improve." No image.*

The second reason matters even more, and it has nothing to do with being nice.

Think about what you do when you make a mistake somewhere that treats mistakes as personal
failures. You fix it quietly. You work around it. You do not raise it in a meeting. That is not
a character flaw — it is a completely rational response to the situation.

Now think about what that means for the organisation. Every hidden problem is a problem nobody
can fix, and it keeps costing money and time and goodwill in the dark.

This is why the language matters. When problems are framed as flaws in how work is designed,
people will tell you about them — because there is nothing personal to defend. A workplace that
hides its problems cannot improve. It's that simple.

## Screen 6 of 8 — Three workplaces, one story

*On screen: three cards, each expandable. Each card names an industry, states what appears to be
a person's failure, and then states what is actually true of the work. The card matching the
learner's chosen industry opens first; the other two can be opened as well. A small decorative
line icon sits on each card.*

**Manufacturing** — A packer keeps shipping the wrong variant. *Two near-identical bins sit side
by side with small labels.*

**Healthcare** — A nurse "forgets" a discharge step. *The discharge checklist lives in a
different system from the one nurses chart in.*

**Office** — An analyst sends stale figures. *Three "final" versions of the file exist and
nothing marks which is current.*

Let's make this concrete, because the pattern looks identical in wildly different places.

On a packing line, someone keeps shipping the wrong variant of a product. Look at the station:
two nearly identical bins, side by side, with small labels. A new packer will make that same
error inside a week.

On a hospital ward, a nurse "forgets" a discharge step. Look at the work: the checklist lives in
one system and the nurse charts in another. Nothing about that arrangement helps anyone remember
anything.

In an office, an analyst sends out figures that turn out to be stale. Look at the folder: there
are three files with "final" in the name and nothing at all marking which one is current.

In every one of those cases, replacing the person changes nothing. Changing the bins, the
systems, the file naming — that changes everything. Same story, three industries. You will start
seeing it everywhere, and that is the point.

## Screen 7 of 8 — Your turn: reframe your own frustration

*On screen: a prompt asking the learner to describe one recurring frustration in their work,
with two text boxes — one labelled "As a person problem" and one labelled "As a process
problem" — and a short single-line box asking which version is more useful. Nothing here is
scored, and a "skip for now" option is available. After the learner writes, a worked example by
an experienced practitioner appears for comparison.*

Now it's your turn, and this is the part of the module that does the real work.

Think of one thing that goes wrong repeatedly in your own job. Write it down twice. First as a
person problem — the way it probably gets discussed today. Then as a process problem — what about
the setup makes it likely?

Then read both back and ask yourself which version hands you something you could actually
change. There is no scoring here and nobody is grading your writing. Take the time.

*The worked example shown afterwards, in the office version:* A person framing — "Dana in
accounts payable keeps paying against the wrong purchase order and it's careless." A process
framing — "The accounts payable screen shows purchase order numbers in a list sorted by vendor
name, and three of our vendors have near-identical names. Nothing on the screen distinguishes
them at the moment of choosing." Which is more useful — "The second one names something I could
change this week: the sort order, or a distinguishing field on that screen."

## Screen 8 of 8 — Takeaways

*On screen: the three takeaway lines as a list.*

Three things to carry out of this module.

First: most recurring problems are built into the process, so changing people rarely fixes them.
Second: framing problems as process flaws is what makes them safe to surface — and a surfaced
problem is the raw material of every improvement you will ever make. Third, and this is the one
I'd ask you to hold onto: your frustrations are data. This course is going to teach you how to
read them.

## Glossary of terms used in this module

**Continuous improvement (CI)** — the ongoing practice of improving how work is done, usually in
many small steps rather than a few large ones.

**Process** — any repeatable sequence of steps that turns inputs into outputs for a customer.
Covered properly in Module 2.

**Special cause** — a source of variation that is unusual and identifiable: one person, one
machine, one day. Contrasted with **common cause**, the ordinary variation built into how a
process is designed. You will work with both properly at Yellow Belt.

**The system** — everything about how work is designed and managed: layout, tools, forms,
software, schedules, policies, incentives, and training.
