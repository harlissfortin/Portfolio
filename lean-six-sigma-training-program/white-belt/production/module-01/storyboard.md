# Module 1 — "Why Processes Fail People" · Production Storyboard

**Module ID:** `WB-M01` · **Seat time:** 15 min (≈7 min narration + interaction)
**Learning goal:** The learner can explain why most workplace frustration is caused by
process design rather than by people, and can name one recurring frustration in their own work.
**Screens:** 8 · **Cumulative check:** none (Module 1 is the first module)
**Built against:** [`../storyboard-and-build-spec.md`](../storyboard-and-build-spec.md)

**Conventions used below.** *OST* = on-screen text (≤ 60 words; never narrated verbatim).
*NAR* = narration (≈140 wpm). *VIS* = visual with its alt text. *XAPI* = statements emitted.

## S1 · Hook — "Was that a lazy person problem?"

**OST**
> Think about the last time your workday went sideways.
> **Was that a lazy person problem — or a badly designed process problem?**

**NAR** (≈105 words / 45 s)
> Think about the last time your workday went sideways. Maybe it was information you needed
> and couldn't find. A system that made you type the same thing twice. An approval that sat
> in somebody's inbox for three days while a customer waited.
>
> Now hold that moment in your mind, because we're going to ask an uncomfortable question
> about it. Was that a lazy person problem — or a badly designed process problem?
>
> Most of us are trained to answer that question with a name. Someone dropped the ball.
> Someone didn't check. Over the next few minutes I want to show you why that answer is
> usually wrong, and why the alternative is far more useful to you.

**VIS** None. Full-bleed type treatment; the question is the visual.

**Interaction** None. Continue only.

## S2 · Teach — The 94/6 rule of thumb

**OST**
> **W. Edwards Deming's rule of thumb**
> ~94% of performance problems belong to **the system** — how work is designed and managed.
> ~6% belong to **special causes** — one person, one machine, one day.

**NAR** (≈155 words / 65 s)
> This is the finding that started modern quality thinking. W. Edwards Deming, working with
> Japanese manufacturers after the second world war, put a number on it: about ninety-four
> percent of performance problems come from the system — the way the work is designed and
> managed. Only about six percent come from what he called special causes: one person, one
> machine, one unusual day.
>
> Two honesty notes before we go on. The exact numbers are a rule of thumb, not a
> measurement; Joseph Juran had earlier put it at eighty-five and fifteen. And Deming was
> making a technical point about two kinds of variation, which you'll meet properly at Yellow
> Belt.
>
> But the direction is what matters, and on the direction the evidence has been remarkably
> consistent for seventy years. When something goes wrong at work, the way the work is set up
> is the overwhelming favourite. The person is the long shot.

**VIS** `fig-94-6` — emphasis figure: hero number **94%** with a horizontal proportion bar,
94% in the accent green, 6% in the de-emphasis gray, 2 px surface gap between the fills, both
segments direct-labeled.
**Alt text:** *"Proportion bar: about 94 percent of performance problems belong to the system,
about 6 percent to special causes."*
**Colour decision (for design review):** this is an **emphasis** figure — one accent hue plus a
de-emphasis gray — not a categorical pair, and deliberately not a two-slice pie. Light
`#0E7A5F` + `#8A9992`; dark `#43B491` + `#6E7D77`. Both pairs clear the CVD-separation and
normal-vision gates (light ΔE 12.9 / 17.1; dark 12.0 / 15.4). The light gray sits at 2.9:1
against the surface, a sub-3:1 WARN whose documented relief — visible direct labels on both
segments — is present. The chroma-floor and lightness-band flags apply to categorical
palettes; a de-emphasis gray is *supposed* to recede.

**Interaction** None.

**XAPI** `experienced` WB-M01/S2.

## S3 · Teach — The mindset shift

**OST**
> **Blame the process, not the person.**
> Then fix the process.

**NAR** (≈120 words / 50 s)
> So here is the shift that every improvement method is built on, and it fits on one line:
> blame the process, not the person. Then fix the process.
>
> I want to be careful here, because this is often misheard in two directions. It does not
> mean nobody is ever accountable for anything. And it does not mean people are helpless.
>
> What it means is narrower and more practical. When you are staring at a problem that keeps
> happening, the most productive first question is not *who did this*. It is *what about the
> way this work is set up made that likely?*
>
> That question is worth learning to ask out loud, because it changes what a room does next.

**VIS** None. Type treatment with the second line at reduced emphasis.

**Interaction** None.

**XAPI** `experienced` WB-M01/S3.

## S4 · Teach — Reason one: it's more accurate

**OST**
> **1 · It's more accurate**
> Swap the person, keep the process — and the next person inherits the same result.

**NAR** (≈130 words / 55 s)
> There are two reasons to work this way, and the first one is simply that it is more
> accurate.
>
> Think about what happens when a recurring problem gets treated as a people problem. The
> person is coached, or moved, or replaced. And then the next person arrives — conscientious,
> well-intentioned, new — and inherits the same layout, the same forms, the same two systems
> that don't talk to each other. Within a few weeks, the same thing goes wrong again.
>
> That pattern is the single most reliable signal you will learn to spot. If a problem
> survives a change of personnel, the problem was never in the personnel. It is built into
> the work, waiting for whoever shows up next.

**VIS** `fig-cycle` — three-step loop: *problem → replace the person → same problem*, with the
loop arrow returning to the start.
**Alt text:** *"A loop: a problem occurs, the person is replaced, and the same problem occurs
again."*

**Interaction** None.

**XAPI** `experienced` WB-M01/S4.

## S5 · Teach — Reason two: it's safer to talk about

**OST**
> **2 · It's safer to talk about**
> Problems framed as process flaws get reported.
> Problems framed as personal failures get hidden.
> **A workplace that hides problems cannot improve.**

**NAR** (≈135 words / 58 s)
> The second reason matters even more, and it has nothing to do with being nice.
>
> Think about what you do when you make a mistake somewhere that treats mistakes as personal
> failures. You fix it quietly. You work around it. You do not raise it in a meeting. That is
> not a character flaw — it is a completely rational response to the situation.
>
> Now think about what that means for the organisation. Every hidden problem is a problem
> nobody can fix, and it keeps costing money and time and goodwill in the dark.
>
> This is why the language matters. When problems are framed as flaws in how work is
> designed, people will tell you about them — because there is nothing personal to defend.
> A workplace that hides its problems cannot improve. It's that simple.

**VIS** None. The four OST lines are the visual, with the closing line emphasised.

**Interaction** None.

**XAPI** `experienced` WB-M01/S5.

## S6 · Show — Three workplaces, one story

Vertical-aware: the learner's enrolled vertical renders **first and expanded**; the other two
are available but collapsed. All three are always reachable — the point is that the pattern
travels.

**OST** (per card)
> **Manufacturing** · A packer keeps shipping the wrong variant.
> *Two near-identical bins sit side by side with small labels.*
>
> **Healthcare** · A nurse "forgets" a discharge step.
> *The discharge checklist lives in a different system from the one nurses chart in.*
>
> **Office** · An analyst sends stale figures.
> *Three "final" versions of the file exist and nothing marks which is current.*

**NAR** (≈165 words / 70 s)
> Let's make this concrete, because the pattern looks identical in wildly different places.
>
> On a packing line, someone keeps shipping the wrong variant of a product. Look at the
> station: two nearly identical bins, side by side, with small labels. A new packer will make
> that same error inside a week.
>
> On a hospital ward, a nurse "forgets" a discharge step. Look at the work: the checklist
> lives in one system and the nurse charts in another. Nothing about that arrangement helps
> anyone remember anything.
>
> In an office, an analyst sends out figures that turn out to be stale. Look at the folder:
> there are three files with "final" in the name and nothing at all marking which one is
> current.
>
> In every one of those cases, replacing the person changes nothing. Changing the bins, the
> systems, the file naming — that changes everything. Same story, three industries. You will
> start seeing it everywhere, and that is the point.

**VIS** Three cards, each with a small line icon. Icons are decorative (`aria-hidden`); the card
text carries the meaning.

**Interaction** Expand/collapse per card, keyboard operable, all cards expandable at once.

**XAPI** `experienced` WB-M01/S6 with the vertical as a context extension; `experienced` on each
non-default card the learner opens.

## S7 · Try — Reframe your own frustration

**OST**
> **Your turn.** Describe one recurring frustration in your work.
> Write it once as a **person problem**, then once as a **process problem**.
> Which version gives you something you could actually fix?

**NAR** (≈90 words / 40 s)
> Now it's your turn, and this is the part of the module that does the real work.
>
> Think of one thing that goes wrong repeatedly in your own job. Write it down twice. First as
> a person problem — the way it probably gets discussed today. Then as a process problem —
> what about the setup makes it likely?
>
> Then read both back and ask yourself which version hands you something you could actually
> change. There is no scoring here and nobody is grading your writing. Take the time.

**Interaction** Two free-text fields (person framing, process framing) plus a one-line "which
is more useful" response. Saved to the learner's workbook, **never scored**. Continue is
enabled once both framings have content; a "skip for now" path exists and is not penalised.

On submit, S7 reveals a **worked model** — the same frustration reframed by an experienced
practitioner — for self-comparison. Model answer (transactional flavour; the enrolled
vertical's version is shown):
> *Person framing:* "Dana in AP keeps paying against the wrong PO and it's careless."
> *Process framing:* "The AP screen shows PO numbers in a list sorted by vendor name, and
> three of our vendors have near-identical names. Nothing on the screen distinguishes them at
> the moment of choosing."
> *Which is more useful:* "The second one names something I could change this week — the sort
> order, or a distinguishing field on that screen."

The model is shown **after** the learner writes, never before — a model shown first becomes
something to copy instead of something to compare against.

**XAPI** `experienced` WB-M01/S7; `responded` WB-M01/S7-reflection with a completion flag only
— **the reflection text itself is never transmitted** (see the data boundary in the build spec).

## S8 · Takeaways

**OST** (verbatim from `modules.md`)
> - Most recurring problems are built into the process; changing people rarely fixes them.
> - Framing problems as process flaws makes them safe to surface — the raw material of improvement.
> - Your frustrations are data. This course teaches you to read them.

**NAR** (≈85 words / 36 s)
> Three things to carry out of this module.
>
> First: most recurring problems are built into the process, so changing people rarely fixes
> them. Second: framing problems as process flaws is what makes them safe to surface — and a
> surfaced problem is the raw material of every improvement you will ever make. Third, and
> this is the one I'd ask you to hold onto: your frustrations are data. This course is going
> to teach you how to read them.
>
> Next up: what a process actually is.

**Interaction** Continue to Module 2.
