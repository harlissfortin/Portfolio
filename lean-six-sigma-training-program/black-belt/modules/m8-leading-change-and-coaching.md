# Module 8 — Leading Change and Coaching (weeks 15–16 · ≈ 8 h self-paced + Immersion 2 (two days, week 15) + one 2.5 h live lab)

Every earlier module produced evidence: a verified cause, an experiment, a control chart, a
future state with its arithmetic. This module covers what evidence does not do by itself —
getting people who do not report to you to change what they do, and keeping it changed.
Rubric items L1–L3 score it, requirement 5 tests it from the reviewer's chair, and Finance
signs it or does not (F1–F2). The methods are diagnostic, not motivational: resistance is
data about the design of the change, and the response follows from the diagnosis. Where
earlier modules ended with "the sentence you would tell your sponsor", this one ends with the
sentence and the decision you ask for.

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this module the learner can:
1. Revise a stakeholder strategy canvas at executive level: for each executive, the cost of the
   change in their own metric, dated evidence of their position, the evidence they trust, and
   the next move with an owner and date.
2. Diagnose a resistance episode as a missing system condition (Kotter) or a missing individual
   element (ADKAR), write the response that follows, and document it to the L1 standard.
3. Negotiate a bounded ask from someone they do not manage using exchange currencies, and route
   a data, interest or values conflict by its type.
4. Facilitate a working session with process moves — observed and scored in Immersion 2.
5. Deliver a 15-minute, one-page executive readout that opens with the decision requested and
   shows the result against the prediction on a chart continuous from baseline.
6. Review a Green Belt tollgate from the reviewer's chair: mark each rubric item evidenced, at
   risk or not evidenced, one sentence per ★ item, the data-ethics line and a coaching flag,
   without coaching in the room.
7. Compute cost of poor quality by category from ledger and time data, classify benefits in
   separate rows, and agree the calculation basis with a named Finance partner before closure.
8. Recognise a problem that calls for DMADV rather than DMAIC and route it.
9. Read a discovered process map from an event log, triage an automation candidate, and state
   the measurement-system and control questions for an AI-assisted step.

## 8.1 Stakeholder strategy at executive level (45 min)

**Hook.** Your Module 1 canvas rated people supportive, neutral or resistant. Fourteen weeks
later the Module 7 redesign touches three budgets and two scorecards, and the executives who
own them were never on the canvas. Their position is not what they say at the steering group.
It is what they release.

**Teach.** At executive level three things change on the canvas
([`../templates/stakeholder-strategy-canvas.md`](../templates/stakeholder-strategy-canvas.md)):

1. **Cost, not attitude.** Replace "resistant" with what the change costs this person in the
   metric they are judged on, in her units. An executive whose number looks worse for six
   weeks is reading her scorecard, not resisting.
2. **Position from evidence, dated.** Attendance, people released on time, the questions asked.
   "Says supportive" and "released nobody" are two different rows.
3. **Move owner and the evidence they trust.** Most executive moves are sponsor-to-peer. Name
   who makes the move and what the executive accepts: their own report re-cut, a walk, a
   peer's result — never your slide.

Each row ends with the position needed by a date and the next move with an owner. L1 scores
the start canvas and at least one dated revision.

**Show (TXN).** The claims stream from 7.2: 2,640 open claims, 410 closed per working day, a
future-state cap of 1,230 with intake control. The registration manager reports a regulatory
service level — "registered within 24 hours of receipt" — and the cap makes claims wait,
counted, *before* registration. Her service level fails in week 1 of the pilot. She is right
to object; the metric she owns says so.

| Stakeholder | Cost of the change (their units) | Position, evidence, date | Evidence they trust | Move owner | Next move, by when |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Registration manager | 24-h registration level 99% → ≈ 70% during pilot | Objected at steering 3 Sep; withdrew a clerk 5 Sep | Her own service-level report | Sponsor (COO) | Re-cut her report as receipt-to-decision, the regulator's clock; pilot reports that line — 15 Sep |
| Operations director | Closures per adjuster flat ≈ 6 weeks while WIP drains | Attends; asks "when does throughput rise?" 3 Sep | Weekly closures report; a walk | Black Belt | Walk the two approval queues holding 1,700 claims; Little's Law on her numbers — 15 Sep |
| Compliance lead | None; gains shorter receipt-to-decision | Not yet asked | Regulatory text | Black Belt | Confirm in writing which clock the regulator uses — 10 Sep |

The compliance lead's line settled the first row: the regulator measures receipt to decision,
so the cap improves the number that matters; the 24-hour internal level was a proxy chosen
years ago for a stream without intake control. The manager's position changed when her report
changed, not when anyone persuaded her.

**Try.** Add every executive whose budget, headcount or scorecard your future state touches,
cost column filled in their units. A row you cannot fill is a conversation you have not had.

## 8.2 Resistance diagnosis and response: Kotter and ADKAR applied (60 min)

**Hook.** The kaizen event installed the new standard on a Thursday. By the second Tuesday
half the team works the old way. Nobody is sabotaging anything; something is missing, and two
frameworks tell you where to look.

**Teach.** Use Kotter's eight conditions and ADKAR as *diagnostic checklists*, not programmes.
Kotter names what the **system** must supply: urgency grounded in the gap, a coalition with
authority, an actionable vision, communication, obstacles removed (systems, metrics,
structures), short-term wins, consolidation, anchoring in what is measured and rewarded.
ADKAR names what an **individual** needs, in order: Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability,
Reinforcement. The order matters — training does not fix a Desire problem and persuasion does
not fix an Ability problem. Find the *first* missing element and respond there.

| What you observe | System condition missing (Kotter) | Individual element missing (ADKAR) | Response that follows |
|---|---|---|---|
| Agreement in the room; old process on the floor | Obstacles left in place — the metric still rewards the old way | Reinforcement | Change what is measured or displayed; remove the old form or screen |
| "We tried this in 2019" | No wins consolidated or anchored | Desire (belief) | Ask which design decision undid it; show the 30-day chart from the kaizen event |
| Attend meetings, act on nothing between | Vision not actionable; no time released | Knowledge or Ability | Is the new task written as standard work? Was time freed, or added? |
| Executive says yes, releases no one | Coalition without authority behind it | Desire (interest) | 8.1: the cost in their units; the sponsor makes the move |

Underneath are three sources. **Interest** — they lose something real; respond at the level
that can change the loss, usually the sponsor. **Belief** — they do not accept the evidence;
respond with evidence in the form they trust, or a trial they design. **Capability** — they
cannot do the new thing under real conditions; respond with design. Persuasion is the right
tool only for belief, and only with evidence.

**Show (HC).** The discharge-before-noon project from Module 1. Countermeasure: a 10:00 unit
huddle confirming each planned discharge's medication reconciliation, transport and paperwork.
After two weeks three of five units have stopped holding it, though their nurses chartered it.
Diagnosis: **Ability**. Pharmacy's reconciliation batch lands about 11:00 because the
technician's shift starts at 09:00 and the queue is worked in arrival order; a 10:00 huddle
has nothing to decide. The response is design — reconcile tomorrow's planned discharges first,
from a list the unit posts at 15:00 — not a reminder. A second episode differs: one charge
nurse objects that earlier discharges empty beds that fill later, so her nursing-hours-per-
patient-day figure worsens while her workload does not. That is **interest**, and correct. The
response is the sponsor's: the productivity metric is adjusted for the pilot period, in
writing, before the pilot continues.

**Documenting the episode (L1).** Five dated lines: what you observed (behaviour, not
attitude); the diagnosis and its evidence; what you did; what happened on the metric or the
behaviour; what changed on the canvas.

**When NOT to use.** Do not run the eight steps as a campaign on one project — a "burning
platform" built from fear is fear selling and fails on the second project. Do not diagnose
from a distance; every row above is confirmed by going to see. When the resistance is right,
change the design, not the person.

**Try.** Write one resistance episode from your project in the five lines. If you have not had
one, ask the team member who is quietest in meetings.

## 8.3 Influence without authority, and conflict by type (45 min)

**Hook.** The decision-rights table says the production manager decides on trial time with
five days' notice. He has said no twice. The table says who decides, not how you get a yes.

**Teach.** Influence without authority is exchange (Cohen and Bradford's "currencies",
awareness level): you can give things people value that cost you little — data about their
own process, time back, visibility with their leadership, a solved irritant, credit. Three
rules: ask for something **bounded** (four hours in Thursday's planned changeover, not
"support"); bring the **evidence they trust**; **give first**, and keep a ledger of what you
have given and asked.

Conflict is handled by **type**, and the first move is to name the type aloud:

| Type | You hear | Route |
|---|---|---|
| Data | "Your number is wrong" | The 1.6 working agreement: go and measure together, one operational definition |
| Interest | "That costs my area" | The cost in their units (8.1); the decision-rights table and its clock (1.5); the sponsor where the loss is real |
| Values | "That is not how we treat patients / customers / people" | Stop; to the sponsor and process owner; never negotiated as a data question |

The five conflict modes (competing, collaborating, compromising, avoiding, accommodating) are
worth knowing for one reason: to notice you have a habit and choose by stakes instead.

**Show (MFG).** The inbound-component project from 1.5. The production manager's daily
schedule-attainment metric books every trial as lost output, so "no" is his rational answer.
The Black Belt asks for the trial inside Thursday's planned changeover (bounded) and offers
first the line 2 minor-stop analysis from the 7.6 OEE work — 132 minutes a day the downtime
Pareto never showed, which he has wanted for a year. The trial runs. Separately, quality and
supplier quality define "component defect" differently, each with data: a data conflict in an
interest costume. The escalation trigger written in week 2 says the Black Belt decides
provisionally and the sponsor ratifies; both functions measure 50 parts against both
definitions before the next checkpoint. Nobody argued about whose call it was.

**Try.** For your hardest stakeholder, write the bounded ask, the currency you give first and
the evidence they trust. Then type your team's last three disagreements and check each went by
the matching route.

## 8.4 Facilitation skills (30 min · practised in Immersion 2)

**Teach.** At Green Belt you facilitated content — the tool, the analysis. At Black Belt you
facilitate **process**: the group owns the content; your job is that the right people speak,
the decision method is known before the decision, and the session ends with owners and dates.
Six moves carry most sessions:

1. **Silent start** — two minutes of individual writing before anyone speaks.
2. **Round-robin** — everyone in turn, "pass" allowed.
3. **Question ladder** — what did you observe? → what does it mean? → what do we do? Groups
   that start on the third rung argue about solutions to a problem they have not agreed.
4. **Parking lot with an owner** — out-of-scope items get a name and a date.
5. **Test for agreement** — "can everyone live with this?", spoken one at a time. Silence is
   not a decision.
6. **Decision method stated first** — consult-then-the-Black-Belt-decides, vote, consensus, or
   the sponsor decides.

Failure modes are the same in every vertical: the facilitator who answers their own question;
the "consensus" that is one person and eleven nods; the session that ends with agreement and
no owner. Immersion 2
([`../practicum/change-leadership-practicum.md`](../practicum/change-leadership-practicum.md))
scores the six moves on observed role-play, pass or redo.

**Try.** Run your next team meeting with moves 1, 5 and 6; have a team member count how often
you answered your own question.

## 8.5 Executive communication and storytelling with data, honestly (50 min)

**Hook.** Fifteen minutes with three executives, one of whom leaves at minute ten. L2 asks for
one page, fifteen minutes, decisions requested and obtained. The chart is the story, and the
story has to be true.

**Teach — the readout** ([`../templates/executive-readout.md`](../templates/executive-readout.md)).
The order is fixed because executives decide from the top of the page down:

1. **The decision you need**, one sentence, first. Whoever leaves at minute ten leaves knowing
   the ask.
2. **The gap in their metric** (the linkage statement from 1.2): where it was, where it is.
3. **What you found**: the verified cause with its effect size in plain language — the sentence
   from Modules 4 and 5, never a p-value alone.
4. **What you changed and what happened**: one chart, continuous from baseline through the
   change to now, with the prediction drawn on it.
5. **What did not work**, in one line. It buys the credibility of everything above it.
6. **The ask again**, with the date needed and what waiting costs.

**Teach — telling it honestly.** These are the data-ethics rules with a chart attached. The
chart runs unbroken from the baseline; a window that starts after a bad month is a
reconstructed baseline. The y-axis starts at zero or says why not; no dual axes. The prediction
sits beside the result, so an unmet prediction is visible and said. n, window and definition
are on the chart. Money appears only in the class Finance agreed (8.7), and an annualised
figure only next to the measured one. An average of averages, a percentage of a percentage and
a 60% improvement from two days are stories that are not true.

**Show (HC).** The discharge readout, one page, twelve minutes. First sentence: "I need two
decisions today: adjust the nursing productivity metric for the transition period, and fund
the pharmacy technician at 0.5 FTE." The gap: discharges before noon 18% against the health
system's 35% objective. The cause: reconciliation batched after 11:00, effect a 2.1-hour median
delay to the discharge order (n = 412, Module 4 comparison). The chart: a p chart of discharges
before noon by day since March, pilot start marked, the 35% prediction as a dashed reference,
result 31% over ten weeks (n = 1,140 discharges, one operational definition throughout). The
line that did not work: the huddle without the pharmacy change moved nothing for two weeks,
and it is on the chart. Boarding hours fell 140 h per month (illustrative, in the observed
range), reported as service impact, no money claimed. Both decisions were made; the L2 note
read "decisions requested and obtained; unmet prediction shown."

**Software.** Minitab Stat > Control Charts with *Stages* on the change date; Excel with limits
computed per stage; R `qcc` with `newdata` for the after period; Python `matplotlib` with a
vertical line at the change and the prediction as a horizontal reference. One chart, labelled
with source, n, window and definition.

**Try.** Read your readout's first sentence to someone outside the project. If they cannot say
what you are asking for, rewrite it.

## 8.6 Coaching Green Belts and the reviewer's chair (60 min)

**Hook.** Requirement 5: you formally review two Green Belt tollgates to the reviewer standard,
seated under a calibrated reviewer's supervision. L3 asks for evidence that you developed at
least one person. Two chairs; the program keeps them apart.

**Teach — coaching.** Coaching is asking. The Green Belt has the problem, the data and the
room; you have questions and a rubric. Three questions carry a checkpoint: *What does the
evidence show?* *What would a reviewer mark at risk?* *What is the one thing you do this
week?* Tell only when the learner is about to break a rule that would cost them (a definition
changed between before and after). Keep a dated coaching log — who, the checkpoint, what they
decided — because L3 scores evidence, not intent. Separation rule: you never sit as reviewer
for a Green Belt you coach
([`../../green-belt/delivery/facilitator-and-coaching-system.md`](../../green-belt/delivery/facilitator-and-coaching-system.md)).

**Teach — the reviewer's chair.** The Green Belt standard is ten minutes, one page, sponsor in
the room, gates at weeks 2, 4, 6 and 8
([`../../green-belt/project/tollgate-checklists.md`](../../green-belt/project/tollgate-checklists.md)),
scored toward [`../../green-belt/project/review-rubric.md`](../../green-belt/project/review-rubric.md).
In the chair you:

- **Read the ★ items first** — M3, A3, I3, C1 — and mark every item due at the gate
  *evidenced*, *at risk* or *not evidenced*, one sentence per ★ item.
- **Record; do not coach.** Your questions serve the record and the sponsor's decision. The
  coaching flag is a topic ("effect size"), not advice; the Green Belt's coach picks it up.
- **Write the data-ethics line**: *none observed*, or the specific concern — points removed, a
  definition changed, a baseline period chosen after seeing the data. At a tollgate it is a
  recorded conversation; at final review it is a stop.
- **Score the project that was chartered**, not the one you would have run; note the Black
  Belt invitation where the cause structure exceeds the scope.
- **Feedback**: two strengths; each ★ item's status in a sentence; the highest-leverage fix
  first.

The hold is the reviewer's most useful output and the one candidates find hardest to give. It
names one missing item and a date; most projects take one.

**Show (TXN).** Tollgate 3 on an invoice-processing project. The page shows a fishbone, a C&E
matrix, box plots of processing time by invoice source, and "vendor portal vs email, two-sample
t, p = 0.03". The countermeasure heading is a new email template. The candidate in the chair
records: A1 evidenced; A2 evidenced; ★ A3 **at risk** — "test fits the question; no effect
size or plain-language sentence; assumptions not stated; not verified as written"; A4 **not
evidenced** — the other four causes from the matrix are absent; the countermeasure targets
email formatting, which was not the cause tested. Data-ethics line: none observed. Coaching
flag: "effect size and the sentence; where the rejected causes went." Sponsor's decision: hold,
ten business days. The candidate's temptation — "the difference is about 1.4 days, here is how
you write it" — is coaching, and the supervising reviewer notes that the candidate did not do
it. The practicum rubric
([`../practicum/green-belt-tollgate-reviews.md`](../practicum/green-belt-tollgate-reviews.md))
scores the record, the questions, the silence and the feedback.

**Calibration.** Before your first live review you score the anchor set — clean pass,
high-scoring fail on an unverified cause, honest-failure pass, data-ethics stop — against the
calibrated reviewer. Any ★ disagreement is re-calibrated before you sit.

**Try.** Score the case Tollgate 3 package alone, in writing, in the reviewer's format. Bring it
to Lab 16.

## 8.7 Program economics: cost of poor quality, benefit classes, Finance (55 min)

**Hook.** The sponsor says the project "saved a fortune". Finance has not seen a number. F1 is
mandatory: without a named Finance partner's signature where money is claimed, the project is
not certifiable at any score.

**Teach — cost of poor quality (CoPQ).** Four categories: **internal failure** (scrap, rework,
re-test, defect-caused downtime, expediting), **external failure** (returns, warranty,
complaint handling, penalties), **appraisal** (inspection, audit, test, calibration),
**prevention** (training, standard work, mistake-proofing, setup reduction). Measure each from
the ledger and timed observation, never from a survey of opinions; the largest part is usually
the hidden factory — rework nobody codes because it is "just how the work is". CoPQ is a
diagnostic for selection (1.1) and a denominator for the benefit case, not a savings claim.

**Show (MFG).** The valve cell from Module 7, one quarter, figures illustrative but derived as
a Finance partner would. Internal failure: scrap 1,120 bodies × $14.60 standard cost (materials
ledger) = $16,350; rework 1.5 h/day overtime × 63 days × $57 loaded (overtime cost centre) =
$5,390. External failure: 41 field returns × $310 (warranty ledger) = $12,710; expedited
freight coded to the cell $8,400. Appraisal: 100% final inspection, 0.5 FTE at $19,500 per
quarter loaded = $9,750. Prevention: SMED and standard-work effort, 180 h × $38 = $6,840. CoPQ
≈ $59,400 for the quarter, of which failure costs are $42,850 — what the process spends on not
being right first time. The hidden part: the 38 s of walking and bag-opening per unit from 7.3
is 4.6 h a day of paid time in no ledger line; it goes on the page as **soft**, in hours.

**Teach — benefit classes and the Finance partnership.** Classes and rules are the Green Belt's
([`../../green-belt/project/sponsor-verification-and-finance-validation.md`](../../green-belt/project/sponsor-verification-and-finance-validation.md)),
with the Black Belt template at
[`../templates/benefit-classification-guide.md`](../templates/benefit-classification-guide.md):

| Class | Counts as | Finance signs when |
|---|---|---|
| Hard | A ledger line that moved over the evidence window | Before-and-after ledger line shown; unit cost source dated |
| Soft | Capacity in hours or units; money only at Finance's rate, labelled soft | Time measured on the project's own definition; rate stated |
| Cost avoidance | Budgeted or committed spend that will not occur | Budget line or commitment dated before the project |
| Revenue | Booked within the evidence window | The booking; never a pipeline |
| Mission / clinical / service | The metric itself; no money unless Finance agrees a rate | The sponsor's executive equivalent, named, signs |

What F1 and F2 score: separate rows, never added across classes into one headline; the
annualised figure only beside the measured figure, computed by Finance, seasonal assumption
labelled; project cost (your hours, time contracts, spend) netted; the no-double-counting
declaration against every project and Yellow Belt mini-project on the same ledger line. The
partnership is a schedule: the Finance partner sees the metric at Tollgate 2 and states the
basis they will accept, sees the evidence window before it closes, signs at closure. A basis
argued at closure is a month's delay, and the Finance partner is never your line manager.

**Show (HC).** The discharge project claims no money. Boarding hours fell 140 h per month;
Finance was asked whether a rate applies and said no — no ledger line moved. The benefit is
classified *service impact*, the Chief Nursing Officer signs as the executive equivalent, and
the project is certifiable. Multiplying 140 h by a bed-day charge would have produced a number
Finance could not sign.

**When NOT to use.** Do not put soft hours in money in the headline; do not annualise a
seasonal process from one season's window; do not claim forecast revenue; do not let CoPQ stand
in for a benefit case — it says what the process costs, not what your change recovered.

**Software.** Excel, every cell pointing to a ledger extract or timed observation the Finance
partner can open.

**Try.** Build your CoPQ table for one quarter by category with the source behind each number.
Send your classification rows to your Finance partner this week, not at closure.

## 8.8 DFSS and DMADV: awareness (20 min)

**Teach.** DMAIC improves a process that exists. When the process does not exist, or the
verified cause is the design itself — entitlement reached, the capability ceiling set by
tolerances, materials or the service model — the route is Design for Six Sigma run as
**DMADV**: Define (charter for a new offering), Measure (requirements to CTQs, with quality
function deployment carrying the translation), Analyze (concept generation and selection; TRIZ
at awareness), Design (detailed design with scorecards predicting capability before anything
is built; tolerance design), Verify (pilot, then transfer with a control plan). The Black
Belt's competence is to **recognise and route**; DFSS end to end is Master Black Belt and
specialist-track scope.

**Show (HC / TXN).** A clinic standing up a pre-admission telephone service that does not yet
exist has no baseline to improve: DMADV from Define. A claims stream whose lead time has
reached the floor of its four-approval design is a redesign question, not a fifth DMAIC
project on the same metric. Both go to the portfolio (1.1) with the route named.

**Try.** One sentence: is your project's next improvement inside the current design's
entitlement or beyond it, and what does that mean for the successor project?

## 8.9 Digital continuous improvement: awareness (25 min)

**Teach — process mining.** An event log (case id, activity, timestamp, often resource) from
the workflow system yields a *discovered* map with frequencies and durations for every path
actually taken — the current state as recorded, at a coverage no go-see matches. Conformance
checking compares it to the documented process. Two limits keep it honest: mining sees what
the system logs, not what happens beside it (the paper workaround, the phone call), and a
timestamp is a measurement system whose entry step needs the same validation as any other
(M2). Mining replaces the *coverage* of the observed map, not the walk.

**Teach — automation triage.** Stabilise and simplify, then automate. Stable, standard,
high-volume, rule-based work is a candidate; unstable or high-variety work is improved first,
because an automated rework loop is a faster rework loop; work needing judgement is assisted,
not replaced. Score candidates as any countermeasure (I2) — impact, effort, risk — with a risk
line for what happens when the automation fails silently.

**Teach — AI-assisted operations.** A model that classifies, extracts or recommends is a process
step. It has a measurement system (a classifier's agreement with a reference set is an
attribute agreement study, repeated over time), common-cause variation and special causes
(drift in inputs, a changed upstream form), and an output rate that belongs on a control chart
(a p chart of override rate; a c chart of escalations per day). The questions are the ones this
program asks of every step: the operational definition of "correct", who checked the
measurement system, and what the response plan says when the chart signals.

**Show (TXN / MFG).** The claims event log (case file below) shows 27% of claims passing
through "request additional information" twice — a loop the documented process, which has the
step once, cannot show. IT proposed adjudication support; triage points at the intake
completeness check that removes the loop, cheaper and lower risk. In the valve cell a vision
reject classifier runs at 96% agreement with the reference set at installation; the control
plan puts its override rate on a p chart, and the response plan names who re-runs the
agreement study when it signals.

**Software.** Minitab has no mining module; R `bupaR`; Python `pm4py`; the simple version is
an Excel pivot of the log by case and activity, which finds the loop above in ten minutes.

**Try.** If your process has an event log, pivot it by case and count activities per case; any
count above one is a loop to explain. If not, write which step you would validate first if
someone proposed automating it.

## Immersion 2 and live lab — run of show

### Immersion 2 (week 15) — the change-leadership practicum, two days
Full run-of-show, scenario cards and the pass/redo rubric are in
[`../practicum/change-leadership-practicum.md`](../practicum/change-leadership-practicum.md);
Immersion 2 replaces the week 15 live lab. Prerequisite: 8.1–8.5 complete; canvas at executive
level; one resistance episode documented; readout page drafted.

- **Day 1 — stakeholders and resistance.** Morning: canvas clinic in trios (the cost column,
  the evidence column). Afternoon: six observed role-plays, twelve minutes each, on cards from
  the case — the registration manager whose service level fails; the executive who says yes
  and releases no one; "we tried this in 2019"; the team that agrees and does not act; the
  values objection; the data conflict with two datasets. Observers score diagnosis before
  response, the type named aloud, the route taken.
- **Day 2 — facilitation and the readout.** Morning: each candidate facilitates 20 minutes on a
  live case question with a planted dominant voice and a planted silent expert, scored on the
  six moves. Afternoon: the executive readout, fifteen minutes timed, to a panel of three
  playing executives, one of whom leaves at minute ten; scored on the decision first, the
  chart continuous from baseline, the unmet prediction shown, the decision obtained.

Failure modes the observers name: responding before diagnosing; persuading an interest
problem; facilitating content; a readout that opens with the fishbone. **Protect the debrief**
— each role-play's debrief is where the diagnosis gets corrected; never trade it for another
card.

### Lab 16 (week 16) — Reviewer calibration and tollgate simulation (150 min)
Prerequisite: 8.6–8.7 complete; the case Tollgate 3 package scored alone, in writing.

- **0:00–0:10 Setup.** Requirement 5 logistics: which Green Belt cohort gates each candidate
  sits, under whom, on which dates (weeks 2, 4, 6, 8 of the running cohort). The separation
  rule stated.
- **0:10–0:55 Anchor calibration.** Each candidate's written score on the case package against
  the calibrated reviewer's, item by item. Every ★ disagreement is discussed until the room can
  say which sentence on the page decided it. Planted: the p-value without an effect size; the
  four missing rejected causes; the countermeasure aimed at a cause not tested.
- **0:55–1:40 Tollgate simulation.** Trios rotating Green Belt, reviewer, sponsor; three
  eight-minute gates from the case's Tollgate 2 and 4 packages (planted: a baseline starting
  after a bad month; a control-plan owner without authority; a soft benefit presented as
  hard). The reviewer writes the record live; the observer counts every time the reviewer
  coaches.
- **1:40–2:10 Finance clinic.** Pairs: one plays the Finance partner holding the ledger
  extract; the other presents the benefit rows. The Finance partner signs only rows they can
  trace.
- **2:10–2:30 Debrief.** How many holds were given, how many deserved, who coached from the
  chair. Name the pattern: the reviewer's job is the record and the one most useful sentence;

**CC8.1.** (M6) A transactional process reports daily mean handling time from about 400 calls
per day. The team expects a countermeasure to reduce the mean by roughly 0.4 standard
deviations and wants to detect that shift within a week or two. The most appropriate chart for
post-change monitoring is:
A. An I-MR chart of the daily means, because the data are individual daily values · B. A p chart, because handling time is compared to a target · C. An EWMA (or CUSUM) chart of the daily means, because it accumulates evidence and detects a small sustained shift far sooner than Shewhart limits ✅ · D. An X̄-R chart with subgroups of five days, because larger subgroups tighten the limits
`[F · B6 · Apply · TXN · S · PRAC]` — EWMA and CUSUM are designed for shifts of about 0.5σ or less; A is wrong because Shewhart limits on individuals need many dozens of points on average to signal a 0.4σ shift, so the chart looks stable while the change is real.

**CC8.2.** (M7) A mortgage stream's daily capacities are intake 90, processing 80, appraisal
review 60, underwriting 75 and closing 85 files. The operations manager proposes adding a
processor to lift processing to 95 files per day. The Black Belt should say this will:
A. Raise stream throughput to 75 per day, the next-lowest capacity · B. Leave throughput at 60 per day and grow the queue ahead of appraisal review, because processing is not the constraint ✅ · C. Reduce lead time by Little's Law, since more capacity anywhere reduces WIP · D. Raise throughput only if closing is also lifted to 95
`[G · B7 · Analyze · TXN · S · PRAC]` — Throughput equals the constraint's rate; C is wrong because Little's Law says lead time falls only when WIP falls or throughput rises, and adding capacity upstream of the constraint raises WIP.
