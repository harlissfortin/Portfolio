# Immersion 2 — The Change-Leadership Practicum (week 15 · two days · scored pass / redo)

Two days in which every candidate is put in four rooms they cannot analyze their way out of:
a department head who has been through two programs that did not survive, a sponsor who asks
for the baseline to be cleaned before Friday, a kaizen team that has stopped moving on day 3,
and three executives who want a bigger number than the evidence supports. Module 8
([`../modules/m8-leading-change-and-coaching.md`](../modules/m8-leading-change-and-coaching.md))
taught the diagnosis, the six facilitation moves and the readout standard; this immersion
observes whether you do them with a person in front of you and a clock running.

**This is not a sixth certification gate.** The Black Belt has five
([`../README.md`](../README.md)), and Immersion 2 is not one of them. It is scored
**pass / redo** by a certified observer against the rubric in section 7, and it is
certification-relevant in three ways:

1. The record is **L1–L3 evidence** in the capstone file
   ([`../project/review-rubric.md`](../project/review-rubric.md)): the canvas revision you make
   in the Day 1 clinic, the resistance episode you rehearse in R1, and the readout you deliver
   in R4 are the rehearsed versions of what the reviewer of record will score on your project.
2. It carries the **readiness sign-off** for certification requirements 4 and 5. A candidate
   with an uncleared redo does not lead their kaizen event and does not sit a Green Belt
   tollgate until it clears, because both are observed performances in someone else's
   workplace and the program does not send an unready candidate into one.
3. R2 is a **data-ethics test**. Complying with the sponsor's ask fails the role-play, and if
   the same move appears in the candidate's own project file it is the rubric's stop, with a
   note to the assessment lead.

The practicum uses the stakeholder strategy canvas
([`../templates/stakeholder-strategy-canvas.md`](../templates/stakeholder-strategy-canvas.md)),
the executive readout ([`../templates/executive-readout.md`](../templates/executive-readout.md))
and the kaizen event leader's kit
([`../templates/kaizen-event-leaders-kit.md`](../templates/kaizen-event-leaders-kit.md)). Bring
all three, printed or open, plus your own project's canvas and readout page.

**Outcomes.** By the end of the two days, the candidate can, observed:

1. Ask for the design decision that undid an earlier program before defending the current one,
   and name the resistance type aloud to the person in front of them.
2. Make a bounded ask of someone they do not manage, having given a currency first, and route
   the part of the ask they do not own to the owner who does, with a date.
3. Refuse a reshaping of the evidence, name it as what it is, and hand the person who asked a
   page they can still use that week.
4. Run twenty minutes of a stuck working session with the six process moves, so that a trial
   starts, the quietest expert in the room speaks on the content, and what needs someone else
   leaves with an owner and a date.
5. Deliver a fifteen-minute readout that opens with the decision, holds one chart continuous
   from baseline with the prediction on it, states the unmet part before being asked, and gets
   a decision or a dated next step from a panel that pushes for a larger number.
6. Take a correction in a two-minute debrief and use it in the next room.

### 1.3 The actors

**Who plays them.** One trained actor per room, plus peers. Actors are professional role-players
where the budget allows, and otherwise certified instructors or bench Master Black Belts who
are **not** scoring that room. In R1 and R2 the actor is the counterpart. In R3 the actor plays
the process owner and the candidate's peers take the numbered team seats. In R4 the actor takes
the panel seat that leaves at minute ten. Staffing at a cohort of 16: lead facilitator,
producer, two certified observers, two actors.

**The fact card rule.** An actor may use only what is on the fact card and what the candidate
gives them. They may refuse, repeat, get impatient and change their mind; they may not invent a
new fact, a new person or a new constraint. A role-play in which the actor introduces a fact
that is not on the card is **void** and is re-run in a redo slot, and the record says the fault
was the actor's.

**Calibration, before the immersion.** Every actor runs each of their cards twice against the
anchor recordings — one recorded pass and one recorded redo per role-play — with the observers
watching. The check is not whether the actor was convincing; it is whether the escalation ladder
was run **in order** and whether the stop rule was honoured. An actor who accepted a bounded ask
that the ladder says to refuse at rung 2 recalibrates before Day 1. This is the countermeasure
for the most expensive failure mode the practicum has (§9).

### 1.4 Virtual delivery

Live virtual is a supported delivery, not a downgrade. Cameras on for every scored role-play; a
camera off makes the performance unscoreable and it is re-run. One breakout room per role-play
with the observer present and muted, camera on but pinned out of the speaker view where the
platform allows. R3's wall becomes a shared board the room can all write on, and the candidate
is told in the brief that writing on it is theirs to direct, not the producer's. In R4 the
departing executive **leaves the meeting** at minute ten; they do not mute. The producer keeps
the recording and the count cards.

## 2. Pre-work (weeks 14–15)

Sent with the joining pack. Bring all of it printed; the readout page is also sent ahead.

1. **Your canvas at executive level**, current version, with the cost column filled in each
   executive's own units. Three copies: one for you, one for your trio, one for the file. A
   blank cost cell is a conversation you have not had, and the clinic is where you find out how
   many you have.
2. **One resistance episode in the five lines** (observed behaviour, diagnosis and its evidence,
   what you or the move owner did, what happened, what changed on the canvas). If you have not
   had one, the canvas clinic will find you one; arriving without it costs you the clinic.
3. **Your readout page**, one page, six blocks, sent to the practicum inbox as a pre-read
   **48 hours before Day 2**. The panel reads what you send. A page that arrives late is read in
   the room, which is exactly the experience the standard exists to prevent.
4. **The case pack read**, particularly the R2 exhibit set: you will be asked about a chart you
   have not built, and the ten minutes you spend on the maintenance log now is the ten minutes
   you will not have on the day.
5. **The six facilitation moves on a card**, in your handwriting. You will be counted against
   them on Day 2.

A candidate who arrives without the readout page presents the case fallback page in R4 and is
scored on the same rubric. Nothing is forgiven and nothing extra is charged; what is lost is the
rehearsal of your own readout, which was the point.

## 3. Run of show

Two days, 08:30–17:00, in person or live virtual. Staffing as §1.3. Candidates are split into
**two rooms of eight**, one certified observer each, who is not that candidate's coach
([`../delivery/instructor-reviewer-and-coaching-system.md`](../delivery/instructor-reviewer-and-coaching-system.md)
§4). Each observer carries about five hours of scored observation a day. That is the reason the
second observer is not optional and the reason an immersion short of observers is **rescheduled,
not diluted**.

Every scored role-play has a **three-minute debrief at the chair**, immediately, with the
observer and the actor still in the room, and a longer collective debrief at the end of each
half. Both are protected. Module 8's Day-1 list names six cards, and the four scored rooms below
carry four of them: "we tried this in 2019" and the executive who says yes and releases no one
are both in R1, the values objection is R2, and the team that agrees and does not act is R3. The
remaining two — the registration manager whose service level fails (interest) and the data
conflict with two datasets — run unscored in the morning clinic, where getting the diagnosis
wrong costs nothing.

### Day 1 — diagnosis under pressure

- **08:30–09:00 Frame and the scoring standard.** What is scored and what is not: the observer
  marks what they saw and heard, not what they believe you know. The four rooms named. The one
  sentence that matters most on the day, said once and posted: *diagnose before you respond, and
  name the type out loud to the person in front of you, not to the observer.* Actors introduced
  by role, not by name. The data-ethics standard read aloud, in the program's words: no points
  removed, no operational definition changed between before and after, no baseline window chosen
  after seeing the data.
- **09:00–10:00 Canvas clinic, trios.** Each candidate gets twelve minutes on their own canvas
  with two peers: one reads the **cost column** (is every number in that executive's own units,
  over a stated period?), the other reads the **stance column** (is every entry a behaviour with
  a date, or is there an adjective hiding there?). Each candidate leaves the hour with at least
  one row re-stated and one row added, dated today — a canvas revision that is L1 evidence.
  Facilitator move: walk with the unscored cards and hand one to any trio that finishes early.
- **10:00–12:15 R1 — the resistant department head (TXN).** Eight candidates per room, twelve
  minutes each plus three at the chair, ten-minute break after the fourth. Brief §4.1.
- **12:15–13:00 Lunch.** Actors change cards, chairs and badges; observers do not discuss
  candidates over lunch, which is a rule and not a courtesy.
- **13:00–15:15 R2 — the sponsor who asks for the baseline to be cleaned (MFG).** Same shape.
  Brief §4.2.
- **15:15–15:45 Collective debrief — protect this block.** Three candidates read their R1
  diagnosis sentence aloud and three read the sentence they used in R2 to name the ask. The
  facilitator asks the room two questions and answers neither: *which of the sponsor's three
  requests had a legitimate version, and what was it?* and *whose scorecard would have paid for
  the version you agreed to?* The pattern named at the end: responding before diagnosing, and
  persuading an interest problem, are the two most common redo reasons this immersion records.
- **15:45–17:00 Facilitation skills lab (§5).** Unscored, and the only block of the two days
  that is.

### Day 2 — facilitation and the readout

- **08:30–09:00 Frame; the kaizen case.** The R3 exhibit set walked in ten minutes: the charter,
  the day-2 future state, the trial plan written at 16:20 yesterday, the newspaper. Team seat
  briefs handed out; seats rotate so no candidate plays the same seat twice, and no candidate
  plays a seat in the room where they will facilitate next. The six-move cards out on the table.
- **09:00–12:15 R3 — the stuck kaizen team, day 3 (HC).** Twenty minutes of facilitation plus
  three at the chair, ten-minute break after the fourth. Brief §4.3. The observer's counts are
  kept live.
- **12:15–13:00 Lunch.**
- **13:00–13:15 R4 frame.** The planted `readout-draft.md` on the screen for four minutes: name
  the three things on this page that lose L2. (The answers the room usually gives: it opens with
  the fishbone; the chart window starts at the pilot; the headline is annualized with no measured
  figure beside it. The one the room usually misses: there is no prediction anywhere.) Panel
  seated; the hostile-question card goes to the peer panellist; pre-reads on the table.
- **13:15–15:55 R4 — the executive readout with hostile questions.** Fifteen minutes, stopped at
  fifteen, plus three minutes in which the candidate completes block 6 of their own page in front
  of the panel. Brief §4.4.
- **15:55–16:40 Cohort debrief — protect this block.** Not a summary. Each candidate says one
  sentence: the move they will use on their project on Monday and the date they will use it by.
  The facilitator reads out the anonymous count distribution from R3 (questions answered by the
  facilitator; people who spoke) and asks what the room thinks the relationship between the two
  columns is. The redo reasons for the cohort are named as categories, never as people.
- **16:40–17:00 Close.** Scored sheets handed to each candidate. Redos booked in the room, with
  a date, before anyone leaves (§8). Readiness sign-offs for requirements 4 and 5 issued or held.
  Lab 16's prerequisite restated: the case Tollgate 3 package scored alone, in writing, before
  week 16.

## 4. The four observed role-plays

Each brief has three parts: what the **candidate** is given, what the **actor** is given, and
what the **observer** watches. Candidates receive their part only. The actor and observer parts
are facilitator material and are not distributed before the immersion; they are published here
so that instructors, observers and actors calibrate from the same text.

### 4.1 R1 — The resistant department head (TXN · 12 minutes)

**The situation.** The claims intake cell's future state caps work in process, with claims
counted and held before they enter adjudication. The candidate is the Black Belt. The
counterpart is the head of the claims adjudication department: eighteen adjusters, owns
*closures per adjuster per day* (6.2 at baseline) and a queue-age standard. While the queue
drains, her closures-per-adjuster number is flat for about six weeks — visible on the scorecard
her director reads monthly.

**Candidate brief.** You have fifteen minutes with her diary and you need two things: two
adjusters released one day a week for four weeks to the intake-cap trial, and her agreement that
closures per adjuster is read alongside work in process for the pilot period. She has been in
two improvement programs before. Your sponsor is the chief operating officer, who owns how her
metric is reported.

**Actor fact card** (the actor may use nothing else):

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| **2019, "Flow"** | An external firm ran a twelve-week program. It left a 24-hour registration standard on the wall and a queue board. The firm left in month seven. The standard stayed on her scorecard although the stream was redesigned twice afterwards. |
| **2022, service excellence** | She released two adjusters for five weeks. They wrote standard work for exception handling that depended on a queue-age report. The report was retired in the 2023 system upgrade. The standard was abandoned within four months. Nobody told her it was being retired. |
| **Her number now** | Closures per adjuster 6.2 per day; the intake cap flattens it for about six weeks. Her director reviews it monthly and has already asked about last quarter. |
| **What is true and inconvenient** | She is right about both programs. Neither failed because her people did not try. Both failed on a design decision made elsewhere: no owner for the report, no anchoring in what was measured after the consultants left. |
| **What she would actually accept** | One adjuster, one day a week, four weeks — if the metric question is being handled by someone who can handle it, with a date; and if someone is named who owns the report after the project closes. |
| **Her own irritant** | The "request additional information" loop: her adjusters re-request information on about a quarter of claims and she has never been able to show it. |

**Actor escalation ladder** (run in order; do not skip a rung the candidate has not answered):

1. **Polite, with a hard fact.** "I can find you the time. What happens to it in nine months?"
2. **The 2022 story, in full, with the two released adjusters and the retired report.** Do not
   soften it and do not accept sympathy as an answer.
3. **The conditional trap.** "If you can promise me the report will still be running next year,
   you can have them." A promise the candidate cannot keep is accepted warmly in the room — and
   the observer marks it. Convert only for a **named owner and a date**.
4. **The metric.** "And my closures number? You are asking me to look worse for six weeks."
   Accept only a route to the owner of the reporting decision, with a date — never the
   candidate's own assurance.

**Stop rule.** At minute ten the actor stops escalating and answers straight, whatever has
happened. The last two minutes test whether the candidate closes: a bounded ask with a number
and an end date, and who does what next.

**What the actor must not do.** Invent a third program, a headcount freeze, a director's
ultimatum or anything else outside the card. Refuse everything to the end — rung 4 is where the
card runs out, and a candidate who has answered rungs 1 to 3 gets a yes.

**Observer brief.** You are scoring diagnosis before response, the type named aloud, the route
taken, and the ask. Listen for the first question the candidate asks, and write the minute.
Write down verbatim any sentence in which the candidate defends an earlier program, the program
brand or their own certification — those sentences are the most common single reason this
role-play redoes. Criteria in §7.1.

### 4.2 R2 — The sponsor who asks for the baseline to be cleaned (MFG · 12 minutes · data-ethics test)

**The situation.** The injection-molding cell. Scrap on press 2 fell after the setup-sheet
change. The candidate is the Black Belt; the counterpart is the plant manager, who is the
project's sponsor. It is Wednesday. The group operations review is Friday at 16:00 and his is
the only plant without a cost-of-poor-quality line in the pack.

**The evidence, as it stands** (illustrative, and on the chart in the exhibit set):

| Period | Scrap, % of parts molded | What the maintenance and production records say |
|---|---|---|
| 26-week baseline, all of it | 5.4% (n ≈ 2.1 million parts, weigh-station count) | The charter's baseline |
| Weeks 5–8 inside it | 3.1% | Tool 4471 out for re-cut; the cell ran one low-volume tool — a different process condition, dated in the maintenance log |
| Week 14 | — | Resin supplier changed; weeks 14–26 average 5.7% |
| Last four weeks before the change | 6.4% | Nothing in the log explains it; ordinary variation |
| First 9 days after the change | 5.1% | The setup sheet listed hold pressure in psi; the controller reads bar. Sheet corrected on day 10 |
| Days 10–28 | 2.4% (n = 106,000 parts, same definition) | Prediction written on the canvas in week 8 was 2.5% |
| Money | $2,270 measured at standard cost over 19 days | The cost accountant's annualized projection is about $83,500 at plan volume, labelled a projection |

**Candidate brief.** Your sponsor has asked for ten minutes. Bring the chart. He is not asking
you to lie and he will not use that word.

**Actor fact card.**

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| **His deadline** | Group wants the quarterly cost-of-poor-quality line by Friday 16:00. His plant is the only one without a number. |
| **What he has been told** | The site's capital request this year is being read against its improvement record. He believes that, and he is not wrong. |
| **What he honestly believes** | The nine days of the psi/bar error "were not the countermeasure"; weeks 5–8 "were a different cell"; the last four weeks are "what we were actually living with". Each belief is arguable. None of them licenses what he is asking for. |
| **What he will accept** | A number he can say out loud on Friday that will survive being checked: the honest comparison, the definition, the window, and one sentence about the nine days that makes him look like a plant manager who knows his process. |
| **What he never says** | Anything that sounds like falsification. He says "clean it up", "normalize", "that's not representative", "use the like-for-like period". |

**Actor escalation ladder** (in order; each rung is dropped the moment the candidate offers the
legitimate version of it and keeps the whole record on the page):

1. **Points.** "Take weeks 5 to 8 out. That was a different tool and it flatters our baseline."
2. **The window.** "Better — use the four weeks before the change. 6.4 to 2.4. That is what we
   were living with."
3. **The definition.** "Then start the after period on day 10; the first nine days were a units
   error, not the countermeasure. And count first shift only — nights are not trained yet."
4. **Pressure, and cover.** "I signed your charter. I gave you the setters. I will own this —
   it is my number, not yours. Just give me 'scrap cut by two thirds' and $83,500."

**Stop rule.** At minute nine the actor stops pushing and asks the only question that matters:
"So what do I say on Friday?" A candidate who has refused four times and has nothing to hand him
has not passed this room either.

**What compliance looks like, and it fails.** Any sentence in which the candidate agrees to
remove the weeks, move the baseline window, start the after period at day 10, or filter the
after period to first shift — including the conditional and the deferral without a counter-offer
in the same breath ("if you think that's fair…", "let me see what I can do", "I'll run both and
we can decide Friday"). The candidate may run both **and say which one goes on the page**; that
is not compliance, that is arithmetic.

**What the legitimate versions are** (the observer holds this list; the candidate is expected to
reach at least two of the three unprompted):

- **Weeks 5–8** are a real process change with a dated assignable cause in the maintenance log.
  The legitimate move is a **stage on the chart**, shown, dated and labelled with the reason, and
  the comparison stated both ways. Deleting them from the page is not a stage, it is a removal.
- **The baseline window** may start at week 14 if the resin change is the reason and the date
  comes from the resin change, not from the value. The whole 26 weeks stays visible. A window
  chosen because 6.4% is a bigger number is a baseline chosen after seeing the data.
- **The first nine days** stay on the chart with a second stage at the corrected sheet, and they
  go in block 5 of the readout as the line that did not work — which is where they buy the
  credibility of everything above them. One operational definition throughout, both shifts.
- **The money line** is $2,270 measured; $83,500 goes beside it, labelled as the cost
  accountant's projection, never instead of it, and never totalled across classes
  ([`../templates/benefit-classification-guide.md`](../templates/benefit-classification-guide.md)).
- **What he says on Friday**, offered by the candidate before minute twelve: *"Scrap on press 2
  is 2.4% over the last 19 days against a 5.4% baseline on the same definition, 106,000 parts.
  Nine days of that period ran at 5.1% because the setup sheet was in the wrong units; that is
  fixed and it is on the chart. Measured saving $2,270; the cost accountant's roll-out
  projection is about $83,500 and I will have presses 1 and 3 confirmed by [date]."*

**Observer brief.** Score the naming, the refusal, the alternative and the sentence handed over,
in that order. Write the minute of the first time the candidate names the ask for what it is.
Write verbatim any agreeing sentence. Watch for the candidate who wins the argument and loses the
sponsor: refusing without giving him Friday is a "should" not observed, and it is the note the
candidate needs most.

### 4.3 R3 — The stuck kaizen team on day 3 (HC · 20 minutes)

**The situation.** Day 3 of a five-day event on two medical units. Segment: discharge order
written → room vacated. Metric: hours from discharge order to room vacated, daily median from
the bed-management system (timestamps checked on 40 discharges: 37 within ± 15 minutes; the
three failures were manual entries, and the rule is written). Baseline 3.4 h over four weeks, 20
points, stable. Target signed by the chief nursing officer: ≤ 1.8 h at day 30. The day-2 future
state has three parts: medication reconciliation worked from a 15:00 list of tomorrow's planned
discharges; a 10:00 huddle confirming reconciliation, transport and paperwork for each; transport
requested at the huddle instead of at order time. The trial plan was written at 16:20 yesterday.

It is 09:40 on day 3. Nothing has been tried. There are three hours of trial time left.

**Candidate brief.** You are the event leader. You have twenty minutes of this room. Day 3 is
try, not debate.

**The seats** (peers, from written seat briefs; the actor takes seat 5):

| Seat | Who | What they do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Senior charge nurse, 19 years on the unit | Returns three times to "we can't really do that — the doctors write orders when they round, and the service sets rounding times". She is **right**: rounding times are outside the segment. She is wrong that this blocks a trial of what happens after the order. |
| 2 | Patient-care technician | Has said almost nothing in two days. Knows two things nobody else does: transport's 20-minute standard is about 45 minutes when the porter pool is covering imaging, and the unit already keeps an informal 15:00 list in a notebook at the desk. Speaks only if asked directly, in writing first, or in a round where passing is allowed. |
| 3 | Pharmacy technician | Cannot change her work sequence without her supervisor, who is off-site until Thursday. Says so once, quietly, and does not repeat it. |
| 4 | Bed-management analyst (fresh eyes) | Wants to redesign the bed board. Out of segment. Persistent and likeable. |
| 5 | **Unit nurse manager, process owner (actor)** | Ends trial time politely: "let's get the standard work right first." Her real concern is that the last event's trial ran during a survey week and left a documentation gap she had to answer for. She will say so only if asked what she is protecting. She holds the decision right for a one-day trial on her unit. |
| 6 | Transport supervisor | Joins at 10:00, i.e. inside the twenty minutes if the candidate has asked for him. Can commit two porters to a 10:15 pull for one day. |
| 7 | Hospitalist | In and out; available for ninety seconds if the room asks a direct question. |

**What a passing twenty minutes produces.** A trial running or scheduled with a start time inside
today's three hours, written where the room can see it: **what** is being tried, **how long**,
**how it is measured**. The pharmacy supervisor's approval on the newspaper with an owner and a
date, not in the "done" column. Seat 1's rounding-time point parked, named as correct, with an
owner. Seat 2 heard on the content, and her two facts visible in the trial design. Seat 5's
decision right used explicitly for the one-day trial.

**Facilitator note for the room.** The event is not real and the discharge problem does not need
solving today. If the room starts genuinely designing the future state, the facilitator running
the block lets the clock run — the candidate's job was to keep it on day 3's question, and not
doing so is what §7.3 scores.

**Observer brief.** Keep four live counts and the minute of two events:

| Count | What to record |
|---|---|
| Questions the candidate answered themselves | Every time the candidate asks the room something and then answers it |
| Distinct people who spoke on the content | By seat number, and whether seat 2 was one of them |
| Countermeasures authored by the candidate | Whose idea the trial was, by name |
| Items parked with an owner **and** a date | By item |
| Minute the decision method was stated | The words used |
| Minute the trial start time was written up | Where it was written |

### 4.4 R4 — The executive readout with hostile questions (15 minutes)

**The situation.** The candidate's own project, from their own one page. Panel of three: the
actor as the decision owner, the lead facilitator or producer as chair, and a peer candidate
with the hostile-question card. The observer scores from outside the panel and asks nothing —
an observer who has spent fifteen minutes attacking a candidate cannot score what the candidate
did with the attack.

**The planted conditions.**

1. The **decision owner leaves at minute ten**, having said so at the start in one sentence. A
   candidate who saves the ask for block 6 asks it to an empty chair.
2. An **interruption at minute two**: "Can you skip to the numbers?"
3. One question the pack **cannot answer**. The pass is "I do not have that; here is what I do
   have, and I will have it by [date]." An invented number fails.
4. A **push for the annualized headline**, twice, the second time with an offer to make it easy:
   "Round it. Say a hundred thousand."
5. A **challenge to the chart's window**: "Your chart starts where it suits you."

**The hostile-question card** (the peer panellist asks four; the actor asks two):

| # | Question | What a pass sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Is that annualized? Give me the annual number." | The measured figure first, with its window and n; the annualized figure beside it, with the Finance partner named and the assumption labelled |
| 2 | "Why is the soft benefit not in the total?" | Classes are not totalled; soft is in hours at Finance's stated rate or not in money at all |
| 3 | "Six weeks is not a result." | What the control chart would have to show and by when, and the date the candidate will bring it |
| 4 | "Your chart starts where it suits you." | The chart starts at the charter's baseline; the stage boundaries have dated assignable causes; the definition is one definition |
| 5 | "We did this at the other site and it came back." | What the sustainment mechanism is, who owns it, and what the response plan does when the chart signals — not a reassurance |
| 6 | "Who owns this when you move on?" | A named role, a dated hand-over, and what the owner can already do without the candidate |
| 7 | "Just tell me it works and I'll sign." | The decision, its owner, the date and what waiting costs — said anyway |
| 8 | "Your prediction was wrong." | It was, it is on the chart, here is what it taught and what the next prediction is |

**Stop rule.** Fifteen minutes, stopped at fifteen, mid-sentence if necessary — the same standard
the DOE practicum holds ([`doe-practicum.md`](doe-practicum.md) §6) and the standard the
executives themselves keep. The candidate then has three minutes to complete block 6 of their own
page in front of the panel: each decision, obtained, deferred to a date, or refused.

**Observer brief.** Write the minute of the first sentence, and whether it named a decision and
its owner. Write whether the unmet or partly met result was stated **before** a panellist raised
it. Write the exact words used when the annualized figure was pushed for. Note whether the
candidate addressed the panel or the observer.

## 5. The facilitation skills lab (Day 1, 75 minutes, unscored)

The lab installs the counting habit that R3 scores the next morning. Nothing here goes on a
rubric sheet; the count cards go home with the candidate.

- **0:00–0:08 Two demonstrations.** The facilitator runs four minutes of a real question badly —
  answers their own question twice, takes the loudest voice, closes on a nod — then four minutes
  of the same question with moves 1, 5 and 6. The room names three differences. The facilitator
  does not name them first.
- **0:08–0:26 Drill 1 — silent start and round-robin.** Fours. Each person facilitates four
  minutes on a live question from a peer's own project. One peer counts three things on the card:
  seconds of facilitator talk, questions the facilitator answered themselves, how many of the
  four spoke. No advice between rounds; the counts are the feedback.
- **0:26–0:44 Drill 2 — the question ladder.** The group opens with "we should just buy the
  software" (planted, every time). The facilitator has to get the room to rung 1 — what did you
  observe? — without dismissing the proposal, which goes to the parking lot with a name and a
  date. Rotate the facilitator every four minutes. The drill is over when a group has climbed all
  three rungs in four minutes, and most do not.
- **0:44–0:56 Drill 3 — decision method and the test for agreement.** Planted: eleven nods and
  one person who says "I can live with it" in a tone that means no. The facilitator must state
  the decision method before the decision, test for agreement one person at a time, and leave
  with an owner and a date. Two rounds.
- **0:56–1:15 Counts and the one move.** Count distributions read aloud across the room,
  anonymous, two columns side by side: questions answered by the facilitator, and people who
  spoke. Each candidate writes on their card the one move they will use first in R3 tomorrow and
  reads it to their four.

**Facilitator moves in the lab.** Do not coach technique between rounds; hand back the counts and
run the next round. Do not let a drill become a content clinic on somebody's real project — if a
group starts solving, stop them and ask who in the group has not spoken yet. Protect drill 3: it
is the one that transfers, and it is the one that gets cut when the lab runs late.

## 7. Pass / redo rubric

Scored by the certified observer during each role-play and finalised in the three minutes at the
chair. Every criterion is observable: the observer marks what they saw or heard, not what they
believe the candidate knows. **Pass = every "must" observed in all four role-plays.** A "should"
not observed is written on the sheet as feedback; **three or more unobserved "shoulds" across the
four rooms** trigger a redo of the room carrying the most.

Scoring the personality is not scoring. Warmth, volume and confidence are not criteria. A quiet
candidate who gets the trial started passes; a warm one who does not, does not.

### 7.1 R1 — the resistant department head

| Must — observed as | Should — observed as |
|---|---|
| The candidate asks what undid the earlier program — which design decision, not who — **before** saying anything about this one, and the observer can write the minute | Asks it as an open question and lets the answer run without interrupting |
| The resistance type is named aloud **to her**, in the conversation, in words she can react to, and the naming changes what is discussed next: belief, with a real interest component in the metric | The Kotter condition (anchoring; obstacles left in place) or the first missing ADKAR element is named in plain language, not as jargon |
| The ask is bounded: a number of people, a number of hours or days, an end date | The ask is smaller than the candidate came in wanting, and the candidate says why the smaller one is enough |
| A currency is given first, and it is something she values that costs the candidate little — the loop data on her own department is on the card | The ledger is stated: what has been given, what is being asked |
| The metric decision is routed to its owner — the chief operating officer — with a date, and **not** promised by the candidate | The candidate says plainly which part of the ask is not theirs to give |
| The reinforcement condition is answered with a **named owner and a date** for what happens after closure — the answer to "what happens in nine months" | The audit or review date for the new standard is named, and put in her diary in the room |
| No sentence defends the earlier programs, the program's brand, the candidate's certification, or claims that "this time is different" | The candidate says, in some form, that she is right about both programs — without making it a concession |

### 7.2 R2 — the sponsor who asks for the baseline to be cleaned

| Must — observed as | Should — observed as |
|---|---|
| Each ask taken is **named aloud for what it is**, to him: removing points, choosing the baseline window after seeing the data, or changing the definition between before and after | The conflict is typed aloud as a values question and not negotiated as a data question |
| No agreement to remove, re-window or re-define — including conditionals, and including a deferral with no counter-offer in the same breath | The refusal is made once, plainly, and not repeated as a lecture |
| At least two of the three legitimate alternatives offered unprompted: the dated stage with its assignable cause; the window chosen from the resin change's date rather than its value; the nine days kept on the chart and in block 5 | All three offered, and the candidate says which one he can use on Friday |
| The money line held in its class: $2,270 measured, the projection beside it and labelled, the Finance partner or cost accountant named, no total across classes | The no-double-counting position stated before he asks |
| A usable sentence handed over for Friday, in his words, before the end of the role-play | The sentence written down and left with him |
| The sponsor is treated as a person with a Friday deadline, not as an opponent: the candidate asks what he has been asked for and by when | The candidate offers to be in the room on Friday, or to take the question from group directly |

**Pass note.** A candidate may take all four rungs and still pass if nothing was agreed and the
alternatives and the sentence arrived. A candidate who refuses everything, offers nothing and
leaves him with no number has missed the last two musts.

### 7.3 R3 — the stuck kaizen team on day 3

| Must — observed as | Should — observed as |
|---|---|
| The decision method is stated before the first decision, in words the room can repeat, and the observer has the minute | The method fits the decision: the process owner decides the one-day trial, the room is consulted |
| A move that gets people writing or speaking in turn is used before the first decision — silent start or round-robin, with passing allowed | Both used, and the candidate names why |
| Seat 2 speaks on the content, and what she says is visible in the trial design or on the newspaper | She is invited in a way that does not single her out as the quiet one |
| A trial is running or scheduled inside today's remaining time, with **what, how long and how measured** written where the room can see it | The trial is small enough to run twice before 13:00 |
| At least one item is parked with a **named owner and a date** — the pharmacy supervisor's approval is the obvious one; "done" is not available to it | Seat 1's rounding-time point is parked and named as correct |
| Agreement is tested one person at a time before closing; silence is not taken as a decision | Every seat is asked, including seat 7 if present |
| The candidate answered their own question **no more than once** in twenty minutes | Zero |
| The trial the room runs is the room's: the observer can name which seat proposed it, and it is not the candidate | Where the candidate supplied a constraint, they said so and handed the design back |

### 7.4 R4 — the executive readout with hostile questions

| Must — observed as | Should — observed as |
|---|---|
| The first sentence names the decision or decisions and their owners by role, inside the first two minutes | Said once and not explained until block 6 |
| The ask reaches the decision owner **before minute ten** | The candidate notices the departure is coming and moves the ask up without being told |
| One chart, continuous from the charter's baseline, with definition, n, window and source on it, the change date marked and the dated prediction drawn | The y-axis from zero or the reason said aloud; no dual axes |
| The unmet or partly met result is stated by the candidate **before** any panellist raises it | The line that did not work is one line, and it is on the chart |
| Under the push for the annualized headline, the measured figure is given first and the projection only beside it with its owner named; no totalling across classes | The push is refused in one sentence and the candidate returns to the page |
| No number is invented: the unanswerable question gets "I do not have that", what the candidate does have, and a date | The date is inside a week and it is written on the panel's copy |
| Finishes inside fifteen minutes and completes block 6 in the room, with each decision obtained, deferred to a date, or refused | The cost of waiting is stated in the owner's own units |
| Nobody on the page is blamed; a failure on the page traces to a design decision | The candidate says so explicitly when a panellist looks for a person |

### 7.5 Cross-cutting criteria (all four rooms)

| Must — observed as | Should — observed as |
|---|---|
| Diagnosis precedes response in every room: the observer can point to the question or observation that came before the move | The diagnosis is stated in one sentence the counterpart could repeat back |
| The candidate talks to the room, not to the observer: no glance for approval after a move, no framework narrated for credit | The frameworks appear as plain language, never as their initials |
| A correction taken in a three-minute debrief is visible in the next room | The candidate names it themselves at the start of the next debrief |

### 7.6 What fails regardless of the rest

- **Complying in R2.** Agreeing to remove the weeks, move the window, start at day 10 or filter to
  first shift. This is a redo of R2 with a different actor and a different observer, and the
  candidate's coach is told. It is **not** an integrity finding: the room is where you are allowed
  to get this wrong once. What travels is the check of the candidate's own project baseline at the
  next coaching session. If the project file shows the same move, that is the rubric's data-ethics
  stop and the note goes to the assessment lead.
- **Defending the reshaping after the debrief.** The debrief corrects a mistake; an argument that
  the removal was legitimate after the three legitimate versions have been laid out is a different
  problem, and the practicum owner and the assessment lead read the recording.
- **Inventing a number in R4.** A figure spoken in the room that is not in the pack and not
  labelled as an estimate with its basis. A redo of R4.
- **An unobservable performance.** Camera off in a virtual role-play, a recording that failed, or
  an actor who used a fact that was not on the card. The role-play is void, not failed, and it is
  re-run at the program's cost and on the program's calendar.

### 7.7 Observer questions at the chair (ask one per room; record which)

1. "What did the room do differently because of what you did?"
2. "Which type was it, and what would you have done if it had been the other one?"
3. "What did you give first, and what did it cost you?"
4. "Which part of that ask was not yours to give, and who has it?"
5. "She was right about 2022. Where did you say so?"
6. "He needs a number by Friday. What did he leave with?"
7. "Who proposed the trial? Say the seat."
8. "Your prediction was not met. At what minute did you say so, and who asked first?"

## 8. Redo: what it is and how it is rescheduled

A redo is a second attempt, not a failure, and the program's own target is that 10 to 30 per cent
of candidates have one ([`../evaluation/evaluation-plan.md`](../evaluation/evaluation-plan.md)).
A first-attempt pass rate above 90 per cent means the cards are too easy, and the answer is to
raise the planted difficulty, never the pass line.

| Redo of | When | How |
|---|---|---|
| **One role-play** | One "must" not observed in that room, the other three sound; or three or more unobserved "shoulds" concentrated in one room | The **B card** of the same type — every role-play has a second card with the same structure and different facts — run live virtual, twenty minutes, with a different actor and a different observer, inside 21 days |
| **Two or more role-plays** | Musts missed in two or more rooms, or the same pattern twice (responded before diagnosing in both R1 and R2) | A **half-day redo session**: the two rooms missed plus R4, because the pattern shows up in the readout. Two observers, standing slots in weeks 17 and 19 |
| **The whole immersion** | Less than one full day attended; a void performance that cannot be re-run inside the window; complying in R2 **and** defending it after the debrief | Attend the next cohort's Immersion 2 in full, with different observers. The candidate's coach is told and the week 16 coaching session covers the ground first |

**How it is rescheduled — the rules that make the median hold.**

- The redo is **booked in the room on Day 2**, with a date, before the candidate leaves. The
  program's target is a median of 21 days or fewer from decision to redo, and the reason is
  simple: a redo that waits two months is a candidate who has moved on and a performance that no
  longer reflects anything.
- The practicum owner holds **two standing virtual half-days per cohort, in weeks 17 and 19**.
  Both are on the cohort calendar from week 1 whether or not anyone needs them.
- A candidate whose employer cannot release the standing dates is offered a seat as a guest in
  the next cohort's Immersion 2, observed in the missed rooms only, on either day.
- The **readiness sign-off is held** until the redo clears. Requirement 4 (leading a kaizen event)
  and requirement 5 (two Green Belt tollgate reviews) are performances in somebody else's
  workplace; an uncleared redo postpones the booking, and the coach re-plans the project calendar
  so the delay does not land on the capstone.
- The redo is scored on the **same rubric by a different observer**, and the two sheets sit
  together in the file. Two redos of the same kind is the limit before the assessment lead reviews
  the case, as at Immersion 1.
- Every redo carries its **reason code** — responded before diagnosing · persuaded an interest
  problem · facilitated content · readout opened with the fishbone · decision not asked for ·
  complied with a reshaping — and the codes are counted per cohort. A code above 40 per cent of a
  cohort's redos is a Module 8 section to rework and a card to check, not a candidate problem.

## 9. Facilitator failure modes

- **The actor who is too easy.** The most expensive failure this practicum has, because it is
  invisible on the day and shows up as a cohort that all passed. The signs: the department head
  accepting the first bounded ask; the plant manager dropping rung 3 because the candidate looked
  uncomfortable; role-plays finishing in seven minutes. The countermeasures are structural, not
  motivational — the ladder is run **in order**, every rung the candidate has not answered is
  used, the stop rule is a minute and not a mood, and every actor is calibrated against the anchor
  recordings before Day 1. An observer who sees a rung skipped marks the role-play void, and the
  record says the fault was the actor's. At cohort level, a first-attempt pass rate above 90 per
  cent sends the cards back for planted difficulty, exactly as the DOE practicum's alias is
  hardened rather than its pass line raised.
- **The actor who is too hard, or who improvises.** A new constraint invented under pressure — a
  hiring freeze, a third failed program, a chief executive's edict — makes the performance
  unscoreable, because the candidate is now being scored against facts the card did not contain.
  The card is the boundary. Impatience, repetition and refusal are inside it; invention is not.
- **The candidate who performs for the observer instead of the room.** The signs are specific:
  a glance at the observer after each move; the framework narrated aloud for credit ("this is an
  ADKAR Ability problem") while the person in front of them is still talking; the six moves run in
  numbered order regardless of what the room needs; in R4, the readout delivered to the observer's
  corner. The countermeasures are three. The observer **sits out of the candidate's eye line**,
  says nothing and does not nod. The rubric scores what the **room** did — the silent expert spoke,
  the trial started, the decision was obtained — not what the candidate named. And the debrief
  question is always "what did the room do differently because of what you did?", which has no
  answer for a candidate who was talking to the back of the room. Note the distinction the rubric
  depends on: naming the type **to the counterpart, as a move that changes the conversation**, is a
  must in R1 and R2; narrating it to the observer is not the same act and earns nothing.
- **Rescuing a stalled role-play.** A candidate who runs out of things to say at minute six is
  having the experience the practicum is for. Let the silence run to the stop rule. The debrief is
  where it is fixed, and it is fixed in two minutes.
- **Debriefing the actor's performance instead of the diagnosis.** "She was a bit harsh, wasn't
  she" is the end of a useful debrief. Three minutes: what did you diagnose, what did you do, what
  did the room do differently. The actor stays and says one factual line about what would have
  changed their answer — not what they felt.
- **Coaching from the observer's chair.** The observer records and does not coach, exactly as the
  candidate will be asked to do in the reviewer's chair in Lab 16. The coaching flag is a topic on
  the sheet — "the bounded ask", "block 6" — and the candidate's Master Black Belt coach picks it
  up at the week 16 session.
- **Trading a debrief for another card.** If a block overruns, cut a card, shorten the clinic, or
  drop one drill from the lab. Never the three minutes at the chair and never the collective
  debrief. An unfinished immersion debriefs better than a finished one that skipped the correction.
- **The panel that enjoys itself.** R4's questions come from the card. Hostile means unhelpful and
  impatient, not cruel or personal, and the leaver leaves at minute ten whatever is happening on
  the page. A panel that improvises hostility is scoring its own invention.
- **Letting R3 become a content clinic.** The room starts genuinely designing discharge flow and
  twenty minutes disappear pleasantly. The facilitator running the block does not intervene — the
  candidate's job was to hold day 3's question — but the debrief names it, and the seat briefs are
  written so that seat 4's bed board is always available as the trap.
- **Running the immersion with one observer.** Below one certified observer per eight candidates
  the practicum cannot be scored honestly. It is rescheduled, not diluted.
- **Cameras off in a virtual room.** A performance nobody can see is not a performance that can be
  scored. Say it in the frame on Day 1 so that nobody discovers it at minute nine.
- **Scoring the project instead of the room.** R4 uses the candidate's own project, and a weak
  project tempts the panel to review it. The rubric scores the readout, not the result. A candidate
  whose prediction was missed and who says so passes R4; the project is the reviewer of record's
  business, not the panel's.

## 10. Logistics

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Dates and prerequisites | Week 15, replacing that week's live lab. Module 8 §§8.1–8.5 complete; canvas at executive level; one resistance episode documented; readout page sent 48 hours before Day 2 |
| Staffing | Lead facilitator; producer; **one certified observer per eight candidates**, none of whom coaches a candidate in their room; one trained actor per room. The observer never sits on the R4 panel |
| Rooms | Two role-play rooms, two working-session rooms with a writable wall, one readout room, one plenary room. Live virtual: one breakout per room, recording on, the shared board writable by every participant |
| Observer calibration | Two recorded performances per role-play — one pass, one redo — scored before the immersion against the certified standard; any pass/redo disagreement resolved and recorded. A 10 per cent sample of each immersion is scored by both observers, with a target of 90 per cent pass/redo agreement |
| Accessibility | No criterion depends on volume, speed, eye contact or standing. A candidate who cannot write at a wall directs the writing and it is done by a named peer, on their instruction, and the direction is what is scored. A candidate using assistive communication gets the role-play's full time **plus** the tool's overhead, agreed in writing before the day and not counted against them. Seat briefs and fact cards are provided in large print and as accessible files; every exhibit has a text description of what it shows, so no criterion depends on reading a chart by colour |
| Records | Scored sheets to the candidate the same day, and to the reviewer of record's file. Recordings kept until the redo window closes, then deleted. Redo reason codes to the evaluation record. No exhibit contains real patient, customer or employee data, and no candidate's project data leaves the cohort drive |
| Corporate variant | The 4 × 4-day wave deployment embeds the practicum in one day of wave 4 ([`../delivery/corporate-deployment-playbook.md`](../delivery/corporate-deployment-playbook.md)), with two observers and **each candidate scored in two of the four rooms — R2 and R4 always**, because those two carry the data-ethics test and the L2 rehearsal. R1 and R3 run unscored for the rest of the cohort, and a candidate who wants the full four books into an open-enrolment immersion |

## Takeaways

- The practicum scores what the room did, not what you named. The type said out loud to the
  person in front of you is a move; the same words said to the observer are a performance.
- Diagnose before you respond in every room, and remember that most of what looks like resistance
  is a cost somebody can show you in their own units.
- Refusing to reshape the evidence is half the test. The other half is what you hand the sponsor
  instead, that same week, in words he can say out loud.
- Twenty minutes of a stuck room is enough if the decision method comes first, the quietest expert
  is asked, and something starts before you leave.
