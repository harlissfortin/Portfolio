# Unit 5 — Improvement in Action: PDCA, Just-Do-It & Your Role on DMAIC Teams (1.5 h)

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this unit the learner can:
1. Run a complete PDCA cycle on a small, local problem — including a real Check with
   before/after data.
2. Choose correctly between "Just-Do-It," "PDCA it," and "escalate to a DMAIC project."
3. Describe what a team member owes a DMAIC project in each phase, and participate
   effectively in a tollgate review.
4. Plan their certification mini-project.

---

## 5.1 PDCA: the improvement heartbeat (30 min)

**Plan-Do-Check-Act** is the scientific method sized for daily work. It is not a lighter
DMAIC — it's the engine *inside* every improvement, from a workstation fix to a Black Belt
project.

**Plan.** State the problem (your Unit 4 statement), the suspected cause (verified as far as
practical at this scale — at minimum, observed directly), the countermeasure, and —
non-negotiable — **the prediction**: *"If we do X, then metric Y should move from A to B."*
No prediction, no learning; without one, any outcome can be spun as success.
Capture the baseline **before** changing anything (Unit 3 toolkit: operational definition,
check sheet or run chart, even a week of tallies).

**Do.** Run the change **small and reversible**: one station, one week, one form, one team.
Note everything that deviates from plan — the deviations are data too.

**Check.** Compare results *against the prediction*, same metric, same operational
definition, same collection method as the baseline. Three honest outcomes:
- Worked as predicted → Act.
- Didn't work → the cause theory was wrong. That's a successful experiment that saved you
  from scaling a dud — back to Plan, smarter.
- Worked, but… (side effects, partial move) → refine and re-cycle.

**Act.** If it worked: **standardize** (update the standard work, train the team, add the
visual control) and consider where else it applies. If not: document what was learned and
run the next cycle. Either way, the cycle count matters more than the batting average —
teams that run many small honest cycles beat teams that bet everything on one big fix.

**The classic Yellow Belt failure mode is skipping Check** — implementing a fix, feeling
good, and never measuring. That's why the mini-project rubric makes "result measured against
baseline" a mandatory item. An improvement without a Check is an anecdote.

**Try (sim, capstone of the simulation thread):** Using your Unit 3 Pareto and Unit 4 cause
analysis of the pizza-order sim, plan a countermeasure with a prediction, run 20 more
simulated orders, and Check honestly. (The sim's default countermeasure is designed to
*partially* work — forcing a real Check discussion, not a victory lap.)

## 5.2 Right-sizing: Just-Do-It vs. PDCA vs. project (15 min)

Not everything deserves a project; not everything is safe to just do. The triage:

| Route | When | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Just-Do-It** | Cause is obvious on inspection, fix is cheap and reversible, affects your own area, no one else's process breaks | Label the bins; move the printer; add the missing field to the checklist |
| **PDCA cycle** | Cause is probable but unproven, or the fix might have side effects — worth one small test | Trial a new intake form for two weeks; pilot a reorder card on one supply shelf |
| **Escalate to DMAIC (Green Belt) project** | Cause is genuinely unknown; crosses departments; data/statistics needed; stakes are high; previous quick fixes keep failing | The 9.8% invoice error rate; chronic late discharges; recurring line stoppages |

Two escalation signals worth memorizing: **"we've fixed this before"** (recurrence means the
real cause was never found) and **"the fix is in someone else's process"** (crossing a
boundary without a chartered team breaks things and goodwill). Escalating is a contribution,
not a failure — Yellow Belts who bring well-stated, baseline-documented problems to the CI
pipeline are feeding it exactly what it needs.

**Try:** Nine scenarios, three per route — including two designed to tempt over-reach
(a "quick fix" in another department's process) and under-reach (a genuine Just-Do-It
someone wants to committee to death).

## 5.3 Being a great DMAIC team member (25 min)

Green/Black Belts lead projects; Yellow Belts make them *true*. You're on the team as the
person who knows how the work actually happens. What you owe each phase:

- **Define:** honesty about the real process and pain ("that's not how it works on nights");
  help scope — what's in, what's out, what will bite the team if ignored.
- **Measure:** the ground truth. Vet operational definitions against reality ("we'd all
  count that differently"), run collection honestly at your station, flag when a
  measurement is being gamed or a sample is lopsided. You are the immune system against
  fictional data.
- **Analyze:** bring causes as checkable statements; volunteer for go-check assignments;
  protect unpopular hypotheses ("can we at least check it?") and challenge beloved ones.
- **Improve:** reality-test proposed solutions *before* the pilot ("that adds three clicks
  at the worst moment of the call"); champion the pilot at your station; report side
  effects fast and factually — bad news early is a gift.
- **Control:** follow the new standard even when the old way is more comfortable; teach it
  to peers; when the new way isn't working, **say so through the response plan** rather
  than quietly reverting. Quiet reversion is how 90-day evaporation happens.

**Tollgates — what they are and how to show up.** At each phase end, the team presents to
the sponsor: what we found, what we conclude, what we need, go/no-go. As a team member:
know which exhibit is yours (often the check sheet or the map — speak to it in plain
language), answer what you know, say "we don't know yet — it's on the verify list" without
flinching (that sentence *builds* credibility at a tollgate), and never improvise data.

**Try:** Video-based judgment: six team-meeting moments (a definition being fudged, a pilot
side effect surfacing, a beloved theory steamrolling) — choose the strongest contribution;
each choice plays out.

## 5.4 Your mini-project: planning it now (20 min)

Certification requires one real improvement, documented on the A3-lite storyboard
([`../mini-project/storyboard-template.md`](../mini-project/storyboard-template.md)) and scored
against the rubric ([`../mini-project/rubric.md`](../mini-project/rubric.md)). Sanctioned
patterns:

1. **5S implementation** (Unit 2): before/after audit scores + photos + 2 sustainment audits.
2. **Standard-work fix** (Unit 2): a documented standard where none existed (or a broken one
   repaired), with before/after evidence of the problem it solves.
3. **Waste elimination via PDCA** (this unit): baseline → countermeasure with prediction →
   measured Check.

**Scope test — "one-inch-wide, one-mile-deep":** completable within ~30 days, inside your
own authority (or one easy agreement), with a measurable before/after. The rubric rewards a
small honest Check over a grand unfinished vision — every cohort someone tries to charter
"fix the ERP" as a mini-project, and every cohort the winning storyboard is something like
*relocating the label printer* with a beautiful baseline.

**This week:** pick your pattern, write the problem statement (Unit 4 rubric), and start
your baseline. Instructor office hours and the cohort channel are open throughout; the
storyboard may trail the exam by up to 60 days.

**Key takeaways**
- PDCA = prediction, small reversible test, honest Check, standardize or learn. No Check,
  no improvement — just an anecdote.
- Triage: obvious+cheap+yours → do it; probable+testable → PDCA; unknown/cross-boundary/
  recurring → escalate with a clean problem statement and baseline.
- On DMAIC teams you are the guardian of reality — in the data, in the solutions, in the
  control phase.
- Mini-project: one inch wide, one mile deep, baseline first.
