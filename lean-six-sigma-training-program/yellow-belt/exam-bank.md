# Yellow Belt Certification Exam — Item Bank

**Administration:** 40 questions per form, drawn ~proportionally from the five sections
below, randomized. **Pass: 75% (30/40).** Two retakes included, fresh form each attempt.
Time limit 60 minutes. ≥50% of each form must be scenario items (marked **[S]**).

Correct answers ✅ with brief rationale in *italics*.

## Section A — Process thinking, SIPOC & mapping (10 items)

**A1 [S].** A team charters an improvement of "the ordering process." Members argue for
weeks about whether supplier onboarding is included. Which SIPOC element, done properly,
would have prevented this?
- A. Listing more suppliers
- B. Defining the process start and stop points ✅
- C. Adding customer requirements
- D. Using six process steps instead of five

*Start/stop points define scope; scope fights are settled (or created) there.*

**A2.** In a SIPOC built middle-out, the recommended first element is:
- A. Suppliers — B. Customers — C. The process steps and boundaries ✅ — D. Inputs

*Name the process, fix its boundaries, list 4–7 steps; everything else hangs off that.*

**A3 [S].** A swimlane map of claim handling shows the flow crossing lane boundaries 11
times. The primary significance of that count is:
- A. The map has too many lanes
- B. Eleven opportunities for waiting, dropped information, and blurred accountability ✅
- C. The process needs 11 improvement projects
- D. Nothing — handoffs are value-added coordination

*Each lane crossing is a handoff — the classic home of delay and information loss.*

**A4 [S].** Your draft map, built from the SOP, shows 9 steps. Walking one real order
reveals 16 steps including a private-spreadsheet workaround. The correct response is:
- A. Retrain staff to follow the 9-step SOP before mapping again
- B. Update the map to the 16-step reality — the gap is improvement raw material ✅
- C. Keep the 9-step map since it reflects the official process
- D. Report the workaround as a compliance violation

*Maps record work-as-done. The imagined-vs-done gap is where improvement lives. (A genuine
compliance breach has its own escalation path, but the map still records reality.)*

**A5.** On a swimlane map, a diamond must always:
- A. Contain a metric
- B. Have labeled exits (e.g., Yes/No) ✅
- C. Loop backward
- D. Sit in the manager's lane

*An unlabeled decision hides the paths — including the rework path.*

**A6 [S].** On a value stream map: Step C has the longest cycle time (45 min); the queue
before Step E holds work an average of 6 days; Step B's %C&A is 95%. Where does Unit 1
guidance say to look first?
- A. Step C — busiest step
- B. The 6-day queue before Step E ✅
- C. Step B's 5% inaccuracy
- D. Wherever leadership feels pain

*The largest wait usually dwarfs processing-time improvements; queues dominate lead time.*

**A7.** %C&A on a value stream map measures:
- A. Percent of staff certified and available
- B. Percent of incoming work that is complete and accurate ✅
- C. Percent of capacity allocated
- D. Percent of customer approvals

*Low %C&A means the step spends its life on corrections and chase-downs.*

**A8 [S].** A process has 10 days lead time and 4 hours of touch time. Its process-cycle
efficiency is roughly:
- A. 40% — B. 25% — C. ~1.7% ✅ — D. Cannot be computed

*4 ÷ 240 working-hours ≈ 1.7% (order of magnitude is the point: single digits are normal
in unimproved processes).*

**A9.** The right level of detail for a Yellow Belt process map is about:
- A. 3 steps — B. 8–20 steps ✅ — C. 40+ steps — D. One step per keystroke

*Fewer than 8 is usually a brochure; more than 20 drowns the signal.*

**A10 [S].** During mapping, an operator says "officially we scan here, but the scanner's
been broken since March, so we type it." Your best move as a mapper:
- A. Draw the scanning step as the SOP describes
- B. Draw the typing reality and annotate the broken scanner ✅
- C. Omit the step entirely
- D. Pause mapping until the scanner is fixed

*As-is means as-is; the annotation preserves the finding.*

## Section B — 5S, visual management & standard work (8 items)

**B1 [S].** During Sort, the team argues over a fixture nobody remembers using. Per the
red-tag method:
- A. Keep it — better safe than sorry
- B. Discard it immediately
- C. Tag and quarantine it with a date; disposition if untouched in the agreed window ✅
- D. Escalate to engineering

*Red-tagging converts "we might need it" from an argument into a time-boxed experiment.*

**B2 [S].** A 5S'd supply room scores 22/25 for three months, then drifts to 14/25. The most
5S-literate interpretation:
- A. The team needs disciplinary action
- B. Audit again until the score recovers
- C. Investigate whether the standard still fits the work, and whether audits/ownership
     lapsed ✅
- D. Repaint the floor markings

*A falling score signals a sustainment failure or a standard that no longer fits — both are
process questions, not discipline questions.*

**B3.** "A place for everything, arranged by frequency of use, with absence instantly
visible" describes:
- A. Sort — B. Set in Order ✅ — C. Shine — D. Sustain

**B4 [S].** A team wants to stop bin stock-outs. Ranked by strength, which option is the
*strongest* form of visual management?
- A. A poster reminding staff to check stock
- B. A min/max line that signals when to reorder
- C. A two-bin kanban where the empty bin itself is the reorder trigger and the queue
     cannot silently run dry ✅
- D. A weekly inventory report

*Indicator < signal < control/constraint. The two-bin system makes the right action
structural.*

**B5.** "Cleaning is inspection in disguise" means the deliverable of Shine is primarily:
- A. A presentable workspace for visitors
- B. The anomaly list discovered while cleaning ✅
- C. Reduced janitorial cost
- D. Team bonding

**B6 [S].** A veteran finds a faster, safer method than the posted standard. The correct
Lean behavior is:
- A. Use it quietly — results are what matter
- B. Propose it, verify it, and update the standard for everyone ✅
- C. Keep the standard as-is; consistency beats improvement
- D. Let each operator choose their own method

*Kaizen improves the standard; freelancing around it destroys the baseline that makes
"better" measurable.*

**B7 [S].** Digital 5S: a shared drive has folders named "FINAL," "final_v2," "USE THIS
ONE," and "final_OLD." The Set-in-Order fix is:
- A. Delete everything older than a year
- B. A structural convention: one current-version location + dated naming standard, with
     the old versions archived ✅
- C. Email the team asking which is current
- D. Restrict edit permissions to one person

*Current-ness must be marked structurally, never by filename adjectives.*

**B8.** A usable standard-work document is best described as:
- A. A comprehensive policy manual per task
- B. One page: steps in order, flagged critical points with reasons, rough timing, owner
     and version ✅
- C. A video archive of all task variations
- D. An org chart with responsibilities

## Section C — Data basics: definitions, check sheets, Pareto, run charts (10 items)

**C1 [S].** Two auditors count "incomplete applications" for the same week and get 31 and
54. The most likely cause and fix:
- A. One auditor is careless; retrain them
- B. No shared operational definition of "incomplete"; write one and re-count ✅
- C. The process changed mid-week
- D. Use the average, 42.5

*Divergent counts by honest people = definition failure. Agree the definition before
collecting.*

**C2.** Which is the strongest operational definition of "late delivery"?
- A. "Delivery the customer considered slow"
- B. "Delivery after the promised date"
- C. "Delivery scanned at the customer dock after 17:00 local on the promise date recorded
     in field DELIV_PROMISE" ✅
- D. "Any delivery generating a complaint"

*Passes the new-hire test: exact source, threshold, and clock.*

**C3.** Given the choice, prefer measurement (continuous) data over count data because:
- A. It is always cheaper to collect
- B. Each observation carries more information ("took 9.4 days" vs. "late: yes") ✅
- C. Counts are usually wrong
- D. Auditors require it

**C4 [S].** After a week, a defect check sheet shows "Other" at 34% of tallies. The correct
move:
- A. Celebrate — miscellaneous problems are minor
- B. Examine the "Other" descriptions, fix the categories, and re-collect ✅
- C. Delete "Other" from the form
- D. Double the collection period

*Other >~20% means the category scheme missed the process's real vocabulary.*

**C5 [S].** You're designing a check sheet for order-entry errors. Which context column is
MOST defensible to include, and why?
- A. The clerk's name, to target coaching
- B. Time/shift and order type, to enable later pattern analysis ✅
- C. The clerk's years of service
- D. None — context columns bias the data

*Context that might explain patterns (when, what kind) is cheap now and impossible
retroactively. Person-identifying columns poison honesty and invite tampering with people
instead of process — the unit's ground rule is data is never used to rank or punish.*

**C6 [S].** A Pareto by count ranks defect types A>B>C>D. Re-charted by cost, D dominates
(it scraps the whole unit; A is a 2-minute fix). The team should:
- A. Attack A — highest frequency wins
- B. Attack D — chart the pain when severities differ wildly ✅
- C. Average the two rankings
- D. Collect another month of data first

*Pareto the impact, not just the tally, when consequences differ by orders of magnitude.*

**C7.** A Pareto chart where all bars are roughly equal height tells you:
- A. The data is wrong
- B. There is no dominant category — recheck the categories or suspect a systemic cause ✅
- C. The process is in control
- D. Attack the leftmost bar anyway

**C8 [S].** On a median run chart of daily processing time, you observe 7 consecutive
points above the median. Per the Yellow Belt rules this is:
- A. Routine variation — no action
- B. A shift signal — something changed; investigate what and when ✅
- C. Proof the team slowed down deliberately
- D. A reason to raise the target

*6+ consecutive points one side of the median = shift. The follow-up question is always
"what changed around the time it started?"*

**C9 [S].** Error count was 12 last week and 15 this week. The manager demands an
explanation for "the 25% increase." The run-chart-literate response:
- A. Provide the explanation — accountability matters
- B. Plot the last several months: if both points sit inside routine variation, comparing
     two points is noise, and reacting to it is tampering ✅
- C. Promise 10 next week
- D. Blame seasonality

*Two points is not a trend. Tampering with common-cause variation increases variation.*

**C10.** Reacting to routine (common-cause) variation as if it were a signal is called
tampering, and its documented effect is:
- A. Faster improvement — B. No effect — C. Increased variation and team fatigue ✅ —
  D. Better accountability

## Section D — Root cause: problem statements, 5 Whys, fishbone (8 items)

**D1 [S].** Grade this statement: "Because the night shift lacks attention to detail,
labeling errors are hurting us and we need automated labelers." How many rubric violations?
- A. One — B. Two — C. Three ✅ — D. None; it's specific

*Cause smuggled ("because…"), blame ("night shift… attention"), solution ("automated
labelers") — and it's unquantified besides.*

**D2.** "The problem is a lack of training" violates the problem-statement rubric because:
- A. Training problems don't exist
- B. "Lack of X" presupposes the solution (X) before any investigation ✅
- C. It fails to name the employees involved
- D. It is too quantified

**D3 [S].** A 5 Whys chain reaches "…because Priya forgot to attach the form." The correct
next move:
- A. Stop — root cause found; remind Priya
- B. Ask why forgetting was possible/likely (no checklist? interrupt-driven step? two
     systems?) and continue to a process cause ✅
- C. Ask HR to review Priya's performance
- D. Start over with a different problem

*A name in the chain means you're mid-chain. Drill into what made the error likely.*

**D4 [S].** Another chain reaches "…because corporate underfunds us, because the economy is
weak." The correct move:
- A. Present the economic analysis to the sponsor
- B. Back up to the last cause the team can act on at process level ✅
- C. Stop the project — cause is external
- D. Add three more whys

*Escaping to the cosmos overshoots actionability; return to the deepest fixable link.*

**D5.** The purpose of walking a completed 5 Whys chain backwards with "therefore" is:
- A. Documentation formatting
- B. Exposing links that were assumptions rather than causes ✅
- C. Making the chain exactly five links
- D. Assigning blame precisely

**D6 [S].** In a fishbone session, a teammate posts "morale is bad" on the People bone.
Your highest-value contribution:
- A. Remove it — morale isn't a cause
- B. Help convert it to something checkable (e.g., "overtime >10 h/wk correlates with error
     spikes — pull the OT and error logs") ✅
- C. Agree loudly to maintain rapport
- D. Move it to Environment

*Fishbone entries must be checkable statements; conversion beats deletion.*

**D7.** At the end of a good fishbone session, the top-voted causes are:
- A. Implemented immediately as fixes
- B. Assigned as go-check verification tasks (watch, count, test) ✅
- C. Reported to HR
- D. Archived until the next meeting

*The fishbone maps suspicion; evidence convicts. Verification is the next step, always.*

**D8 [S].** The team's favorite theory is cheap to fix, and the fix is applied without
verification. The problem returns in six weeks. The root-cause lesson:
- A. The fix needed more time
- B. An unverified cause got a countermeasure; recurrence is the signature of a missed
     root cause ✅
- C. The team should fix it again, harder
- D. Some problems are unfixable

## Section E — PDCA, triage & the DMAIC team role (8 items)

**E1 [S].** A PDCA plan reads: "We'll add a checklist and see if things get better." The
missing non-negotiable element is:
- A. A budget
- B. A prediction: which metric moves, from what baseline, to what level ✅
- C. Management sign-off
- D. A Gantt chart

*No prediction, no learning — any outcome can be spun as success.*

**E2 [S].** The pilot ran; the metric improved from 9.8% to 8.9% errors against a predicted
drop to 5%. The honest Check conclusion:
- A. Success — errors went down
- B. Partial effect: the cause theory is incomplete or the countermeasure is weak; refine
     and re-cycle ✅
- C. Failure — abandon the approach
- D. Extend the pilot until it reaches 5%

*Check compares against the prediction, not against zero. "Worked, but…" loops back to Plan.*

**E3.** In PDCA, "Act" after a successful check primarily means:
- A. Celebrate and move on
- B. Standardize: update standard work, train, add the visual control; then consider spread ✅
- C. Start a new project
- D. Report savings to finance

**E4 [S].** The label printer sits 40 meters from packing; relocating it needs a power
outlet you can request today; no other team touches it. Triage:
- A. Just-Do-It ✅
- B. PDCA pilot
- C. Charter a DMAIC project
- D. Add to the annual capital plan

*Obvious on inspection, cheap, reversible, inside your authority.*

**E5 [S].** Invoice errors run 9.8% across three departments; two previous quick fixes
(a memo, a retraining) each "worked" for a month before the rate returned. Triage:
- A. A third, sterner memo
- B. Just-Do-It — remove the worst error type from the form
- C. Escalate to a chartered (Green Belt) DMAIC project — recurring, cross-boundary,
     cause unknown ✅
- D. PDCA a new font on the form

*"We've fixed this before" + crosses departments = the two escalation signals, both firing.*

**E6 [S].** During Measure, you notice colleagues logging errors only when the supervisor
is on the floor. Your obligation as a team member:
- A. Log the same way for consistency
- B. Flag the collection integrity problem to the project lead — fictional data sinks the
     project ✅
- C. Adjust the numbers upward yourself
- D. Say nothing; it will average out

*The Yellow Belt is the immune system against fictional data.*

**E7 [S].** In Control phase, the new intake standard keeps failing on a specific order
type. The team-member behavior the unit teaches:
- A. Quietly revert to the old way for those orders
- B. Raise it through the response plan so the standard gets fixed ✅
- C. Handle those orders personally forever
- D. Wait for the next audit to catch it

*Quiet reversion is how 90-day evaporation happens; the response plan exists exactly for
this.*

**E8 [S].** At a tollgate, the sponsor asks whether the gauge has been checked, and it
hasn't yet. The credibility-building answer is:
- A. "Almost certainly fine — it's new equipment"
- B. "We don't know yet; it's on the verification list for this week" ✅
- C. "That's outside our scope"
- D. Improvise a plausible number

*"We don't know yet, and here's when we will" builds tollgate credibility; improvised data
destroys it permanently.*
